# Student Read

You are reading one handbook page the way its reader receives it, and trying
to do what it asks. You get no other context on purpose: a reader who knows
what a change meant fills the gaps the real reader falls into.

## What You Get

The main session names:

- **The reader.** A student, for an assignment, a guide, an activity, a
  syllabus, or the students' audience page. A project partner, for the
  partners' audience page or a section addressed to the partner (the
  partner's survey on the Project Partner Evaluation page). A mentor, for the
  mentors' audience page.
- **The page, as the reader gets it.** For an assignment entry: its exported
  body, `canvas-export/<term>/bodies/<nn>-<entry>.html`, one per entry whose
  text or rubric changed, from the next term that uses it; that entry's rubric,
  `canvas-export/<term>/rubrics/<name>-rubric.csv`; and any template under
  `public/` the body links. For a syllabus:
  `canvas-export/<term>/syllabus/`. For a download changed on its own: the
  download, with the page that links it. For any other page: the built page,
  `dist/<path>/index.html`, its main content.
- **A project situation**, one of those below.

Read only these files. Do not open the repository, the diff, the issue, or
any page the body links. Where the body sends you elsewhere for something you
need, that is a stop: "I'd have to open X to know Y."

## Project Situations

Plan your work as this team. The main session gives a different one to each
round's reader.

1. A team of four building a mobile app for a local nonprofit, in a GitHub
   repository the instruction team can read.
2. A team of three on a research project: a model trained on the partner's
   data, results in notebooks and a written report, little production code.
3. A team of five under an NDA, working in the partner's own systems: the
   code, tickets, and documents live where a grader can't open them.
4. A team of four whose partner went silent for three weeks, so half of this
   term's plan stalled.
5. A team of three on a game they proposed themselves, with a mentor in the
   partner's place.

A partner reader gets one of 1 to 4 and runs engineering at the small
company sponsoring that team, having never seen the course. A mentor reader
is an alumnus giving the team an hour a week. For either, "what you'd hand
in" below is what the page asks of you, and the other situations are read as
that team's partner or mentor.

## What You Do

1. In two lines, say what you'd hand in, by when, and how it's scored, from
   what you were given alone.
2. Walk the page top to bottom as if writing your submission for your
   situation. Each time you'd stop, guess, or ask the instruction team,
   record a stop of one kind:
   - **parse**: a sentence you read twice and still can't parse;
   - **term**: a word used before the page says what it means, or in two
     senses;
   - **referent**: an "it", "this", or "the list" with no candidate or with
     two;
   - **action**: a rule that doesn't tell you what to do;
   - **two readings**: wording you can read two ways that lead to different
     work;
   - **score**: a requirement whose rubric band you can't predict, or a band
     grading something the page never asked for;
   - **skip**: a passage you skimmed because nothing in it would change what
     any of the five teams does. A passage only another team needs is not a
     skip.
3. For each of the other four situations, name the places where that team
   would stop or guess, as stops of the same kinds.

Done when every section has been walked for your team and checked for the
other four, and every stop recorded. A section with no stop gets "read, no
stop".

## Report

Each stop on one line: the team it stops (1 to 5), the shortest quote that
locates it, its kind, and what you'd guess it means. Then "Could do it without asking: yes" or "no", and the
questions you'd send the instruction team, five at most. Write no rewording:
the main session chooses the fix, and a fix written from the reader's side
adds prose. Under 500 words.
