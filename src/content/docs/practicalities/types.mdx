Every project has one of four **outcome types**. The outcome type says what kind of work it is, who stands behind it, and which shipping playbook applies. It does not change the grade: every project is scored on the same outcome ladder, who ran the software, and the outcome type only says what each rung looks like for you.

A project that fits no outcome type still has a partner or mentor and a target rung. Your [Definition of Shipped](/assignments/definition-of-shipped/) is where you write down what that rung means for it.

## FOSS

Contribute patches to an existing Free and Open-Source Software project. Examples include patches for the Rust compiler, the Xen hypervisor, the Habitica todo-list game, or the OSU Open Source Lab repositories. Established projects publish the features and fixes they want help with, so the opportunity is already defined; the work is earning a maintainer's review and approval, which is a relationship, not a step.

**What you need:** a project mentor (required for FOSS); a working build of the upstream project for every member; the project's contribution workflow (CLA or DCO, style, commit conventions, CI) followed from the first pull request.

## Research

Collaborate with a professor on a research question, aiming for results someone else can reproduce and, when they hold up, a small paper. Ideal if you are considering graduate school; the teamwork component can be met through significant interaction with the professor's research group. The professor owns the question and knows the approvals it needs; your team owns the pipeline, the reproduced baseline, and the artifact.

**What you need:** a faculty partner; data you are allowed to use, which can mean an IRB determination or a data use agreement filed in fall; a reproducible artifact from the first run onward.

## Consultancy

Build software for a specific external partner: a citizen-science hive-tracking app for a nonprofit, a study tool for a professor in the School of Psychological Science, an internal tool for a company. The partner defines success and, at the top of the ladder, runs the system in their own environment, so the hardest prerequisites are theirs: IT and security review, accounts, a handoff their people can operate.

**What you need:** a project partner who can accept and run the deliverable; acceptance criteria the partner endorsed before you built; the partner's approval path mapped in fall, with dates.

## New Product or Game

Create something new, which may or may not become a viable business. You define the opportunity, which is exciting and uncertain in equal measure. The ladder counts people who do not know you using the thing, and strangers take months to find, so the recruitment channel is a workstream from fall, not a spring task.

**What you need:** a project mentor (required for New Product or Game); a deployed walking skeleton by the fall week 10 gate; a recruitment channel (a subreddit, a Discord, a campus group, an itch.io page) with its own backlog items by winter; telemetry that has been quietly collecting since before you needed it.

If you want to take the product further than the course, [Startup School](https://www.startupschool.org/) is a free curriculum from Y Combinator, and the [OSU Advantage Accelerator](https://advantage.oregonstate.edu/advantage-accelerator) is the campus program; its [Iterate workshop](/activities/requirements/#osu-advantage-accelerators-iterate-program) is in the activity library. Neither replaces a deployed URL and a dozen strangers using it.
