Read this in your project's first week, before you agree a target with your partner; it gives you four paths up the ladder and the clocks to start now.

"Shipped" is not a single state, and the most useful way to think about it is as a ladder measured by one question: who ran the software. At the bottom, nobody outside the team ran it. One rung up, your partner watched it run. Above that the rungs depend on what kind of project you have, and the top of the ladder always means the same thing in different words: people who are not you depend on it. The top rungs are hard, and they are hard for reasons that have little to do with how good your code is.

Here is the fact that should reorganize your year: with AI tools, building is rarely the long pole anymore. **The external world is.** Maintainer review latency, app store review, IRB approval and data access, partner IT security review, recruiting users you don't know: every one of these clocks is measured in weeks, none of them is under your control, and none of them can be compressed by working harder in the final month. The only move that works is starting the clocks early. That means choosing the rung you are aiming for at the very beginning, planning backward from it, and starting the slow external processes in your first week rather than when you feel ready for them.

This guide is the playbook. Each section below is a worked example of one path up the ladder: what each rung means for that kind of project, the prerequisites rung by rung, a timeline in three phases (early, middle, and late, each roughly a third of the project), the common blockers, and the evidence worth collecting as you go. Evidence is the part teams leave until last and regret: a merged pull request, a dashboard, a maintainer's comment and a partner's email are all trivial to capture the day they happen and painful to reconstruct afterwards.

**What shipped means for your project is something you and your project partner or mentor agree**, not a label anyone assigns you. FOSS, New Product or Game, Research and Consultancy are the paths teams have most often taken, and they are here to give you an idea of what to expect rather than to sort you into a box. **A project that looks like none of them is fully fine**, and writing your own expectations costs you nothing, because what matters is the rung you commit to rather than which example you resemble: either way, you need a rung you are aiming at and a partner who agreed it. Read the section closest to your project, then take the others as a menu, because the lead times in one routinely bite a project in another.

## FOSS

**What it is:** you contribute patches to an existing free and open-source project rather than starting your own: the [Rust compiler](https://github.com/rust-lang/rust), the [Xen hypervisor](https://xenproject.org/), the [Habitica](https://habitica.com/) todo-list game, a repository from the [OSU Open Source Lab](https://osuosl.org/). The real work is earning a maintainer's review and approval, and that is a relationship you build rather than a step you execute.

**On the ladder:** for FOSS the rungs read, from the bottom, a pull request submitted; reviewed and revised; merged; shipped in an upstream release. Note what separates submitted from merged: not your code, but a maintainer's attention and approval. That is a resource you must cultivate, not a step you execute.

**Rung-by-rung prerequisites:**

- **Submitted:** a working development setup for the upstream project, their contribution workflow understood (a Contributor License Agreement or the lighter-weight [Developer Certificate of Origin](https://developercertificate.org/) signed, style and commit conventions followed, tests passing in their CI).
- **Reviewed:** a maintainer chose to spend time on your PR. Review latency on large projects is commonly 2 to 8 weeks per round, and most substantive PRs take multiple rounds.
- **Merged:** every review round addressed, rebased onto a moving main branch, and the change judged worth maintaining forever by people who will own it after you graduate.

Aim to have a first upstream pull request submitted in the early phase, and aim past that: **get a trivial PR merged in the same phase.** A typo fix, a docs clarification, a good first issue. Its purpose is not the diff; it is opening the maintainer channel, proving your toolchain end to end (fork, build, test, CLA, CI, review etiquette), and teaching you this project's review culture while the stakes are near zero.

Communication norms matter as much as code. Read the project's [CONTRIBUTING file](https://docs.github.com/en/communities/setting-up-your-project-for-healthy-contributions/setting-guidelines-for-repository-contributors) and recent merged PRs before writing anything. GitHub's [Open Source Guides](https://opensource.guide/how-to-contribute/) cover the etiquette in more detail than most projects write down. Discuss substantial changes in an issue or mailing list **before** building them; an unsolicited large PR is the most-rejected artifact in open source. Be responsive when review arrives: maintainers deprioritize contributors who take weeks to respond.

| Phase | What must be true |
|---|---|
| Early | Upstream builds and tests locally for every member; CLA/DCO done; first PR submitted, ideally a trivial one merged; target issues discussed with maintainers |
| Middle | Substantive PR(s) submitted and in active review; at least one review round addressed |
| Late | PRs merged; follow-up fixes handled; contribution noted in changelog or release notes if possible |

**Common blockers:** review latency (weeks per round, worse around releases); CLA requiring employer or university sign-off; a maintainer disagreeing with your approach after you built it (discuss first); the project migrating or freezing mid-year; your patch conflicting with a moving main branch.

**Evidence to collect:** links to merged and in-review PRs, the review threads themselves (they prove engagement), issue discussions where maintainers endorsed your direction, changelog or release mentions, and any maintainer comments praising the work. Screenshot or archive these as they happen.

## New Product or Game

**What it is:** you create something that did not exist, which may or may not turn into a viable business. The distinguishing feature is that the people at the top of your ladder do not know you, and strangers take months to find rather than weeks.

**On the ladder:** for a new product or game the rungs read, from the bottom, friends tried a deployed build; a dozen strangers, or a completed user study; two dozen strangers; sustained use by tens of heavy or hundreds of light users. The jump from friends to a dozen strangers is the hard one: strangers cannot try software that is not deployed, and they will not arrive the week you need them.

**Rung-by-rung prerequisites:**

- **Friends tried it:** a deployed URL or installable build. Nothing more, but nothing less: "clone the repo and run it" does not count as trying your product.
- **A dozen strangers:** a production URL with a real domain and TLS, an onboarding flow a stranger survives without you on a call, a recruitment channel (subreddit, Discord, campus group, itch.io page), and telemetry so you can prove they came.
- **Two dozen strangers, and beyond:** retention, which means fixing what the first dozen users hit, which means having had those users early enough to iterate.

**Deploy the walking skeleton to a production URL in the early phase.** It flushes out the whole external dependency chain (domain registration, DNS, TLS, hosting account limits, billing) while there is nothing to lose. See the [DevOps guide](/guides/devops/) for how.

Know your **store clocks** if you are distributing through one. Apple: developer account approval can take days, and [Apple reviews about 90% of submissions in under 24 hours](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/), but that is the clock on one round. Rejections and resubmission loops are routine, first submissions get extra scrutiny, and review slows near holidays. Google Play: new personal accounts face a testing requirement (a closed test with a dozen testers over two weeks) before production access, and the [test tracks](https://developer.android.com/distribute/best-practices/launch/test-tracks) are worth understanding before you need them. For games, **[itch.io](https://itch.io/developers) is the fast path**: no review queue, upload and publish the same day. Ship on itch.io early even if a store launch is the end goal.

Add an **analytics or telemetry starter early**, not in the last phase: a privacy-respecting page-view and event counter ([Plausible](https://plausible.io/), [Umami](https://umami.is/), [PostHog](https://posthog.com/)) or a simple event log. Any credible claim about use requires metrics, and metrics require instrumentation that has been quietly collecting since before you needed it.

If the venture question interests you beyond the software, [Y Combinator's Startup School](https://www.startupschool.org/) is a free, self-paced course on taking a product to a market, and the [OSU Advantage Accelerator](https://advantage.oregonstate.edu/advantage-accelerator) runs programs for student founders. Neither is a substitute for a dozen strangers using the thing: they help you decide what to build next, and the ladder only counts what someone actually ran.

| Phase | What must be true |
|---|---|
| Early | Production URL live (walking skeleton); domain, TLS, hosting sorted; analytics wired; store accounts created if relevant |
| Middle | Beta users onboarding themselves; feedback loop running; store test track passed if relevant; real-user session evidenced |
| Late | Strangers using it; metrics dashboard showing it; store listing live or study executed |

**Common blockers:** app store rejection loops, Play Console testing requirements, free-tier hosting limits hit at the worst moment, domain or account ownership stuck with one team member, and above all user recruitment: strangers take weeks to find, so the channel must exist by the middle phase.

**Evidence to collect:** the production URL and uptime history, analytics screenshots with dates, user counts and testimonials, store listing links and review correspondence, user study protocol and results, and retention evidence: repeat-use counts or week-over-week active users, which is what separates the top rungs from a launch spike.

## Research

**What it is:** you collaborate with a professor on a research question, aiming at a result someone else can reproduce and, when it holds up, a paper. It is the natural path if you are considering graduate school, and the teamwork you need can come from sustained contact with the professor's research group rather than only from your own team.

**On the ladder:** for research the rungs read, from the bottom, a real-data run shown to your partner; your partner's group ran it on real data and you fixed what they found; someone outside the team reran the pipeline and got your numbers; a stranger reproduced the result from the published artifact, which is what a paper is for. Underneath every rung sits an unstated prerequisite: **data you are allowed to use** and **results someone else can reproduce**.

**Rung-by-rung prerequisites:**

- **A pipeline first:** it runs end to end on sample data by the end of the early phase, the first gate the rest depends on.
- **A real-data run:** the real dataset, which means data access agreements and possibly IRB approval, both of which are clocks you do not control.
- **Reran by others, then reproduced by a stranger:** the reproducible artifact described below, a reproduced baseline to compare against, compute for larger runs, and enough calendar time to iterate after the first results disappoint you (they will).

**Start the IRB and data-access clocks in your first week.** [Institutional Review Board review at OSU](https://research.oregonstate.edu/irb) takes weeks even for exempt determinations, and longer if revisions are requested; data use agreements between institutions can take months. Your faculty partner knows these processes: ask in your first meeting what approvals the project needs and who files them. If human subjects are even plausibly involved (user studies count), ask explicitly.

Get the pipeline running end to end on sample data in the early phase. Clearing that bar early means that when real data arrives, you are debugging science instead of plumbing.

**Reproducibility is the bar.** A result that only exists on one student's laptop is a rumor. This is not a classroom affectation: a [Nature survey of 1,576 researchers](https://www.nature.com/articles/533452a) found that more than 70% had tried and failed to reproduce another scientist's experiment, and the response across computing has been to make the artifact, not just the paper, the reviewable object. A reproducible artifact means: pinned dependencies (a lock file or container), versioned or precisely referenced data, seeded runs, one documented command from clean checkout to result table, and a README a stranger can follow. The test is literal: another team member, or your professor's grad student, runs it fresh and gets your numbers. **Reproduce the baseline before you attempt novelty**: if you cannot reproduce the published number you are comparing against, you cannot claim to beat it.

| Phase | What must be true |
|---|---|
| Early | IRB/data-access filings submitted; pipeline runs end to end on sample data; baseline identified and reproduction started |
| Middle | Real data in hand; baseline reproduced; first real experiments run; results tracked and versioned |
| Late | Novel experiments complete; artifact reproducible by a stranger; paper or report drafted with your partner |

**Common blockers:** IRB revision cycles, data agreements stalled in legal review, the baseline paper's code not actually running, compute quotas, and the classic: results that arrive a few weeks before the end with no time left to interpret them.

**Evidence to collect:** approval letters and agreement dates, the reproducible artifact itself (repo tag plus instructions), baseline reproduction numbers next to published ones, experiment logs, your partner's written confirmation that results are novel or submission-worthy, and the paper draft or preprint link.

## Consultancy

**What it is:** you build software for one specific external partner: a citizen-science application for a nonprofit, a study tool for a professor in another department, an internal tool for a company. At the top of the ladder the partner runs the system in their own environment, which means the hardest prerequisites on your list are theirs to satisfy, not yours.

**On the ladder:** for consultancy the rungs read, from the bottom, the partner tried a prototype; their feedback addressed in a newer version; the partner integrating it into production; the partner running it in operations. Read the top rungs carefully: they describe actions the **partner** takes, not you. Your job is to make those actions easy, safe, and early.

**Rung-by-rung prerequisites:**

- **Feedback on a prototype, then addressed:** something deployed where the partner can click it, early enough that their feedback can shape the next version.
- **Partner integrating:** the system running in the **partner's** environment, which means their IT department has said yes: accounts provisioned, security review passed, data handling approved.
- **In production:** all of the above plus a handoff the partner's people can operate without you.

**Ask about IT and security review in your first meeting with the partner.** "Who has to approve software running in your environment, and how long does that take?" At small nonprofits the answer may be "the director, tomorrow." At hospitals, school districts, government agencies, and large companies, the answer is a formal review measured in **months**, sometimes with penetration tests, data protection assessments, or vendor onboarding forms. Whatever the answer, write it into your plan with an owner and a start-by date.

**Agree on acceptance criteria early and in writing.** The "feedback addressed" rung is only checkable against criteria the partner endorsed before you built. Vague scope is how consultancy projects reach the late phase with a partner who feels the system "isn't what we asked for."

Stage the deployment: your own hosting first (fast iteration, demos), then a **staging instance in the partner's environment** in the middle phase, then production in the late one. The staging step is the one teams skip and regret; it is where authentication schemes, network policies, and data access rules surface.

Finally, adopt the **handoff-is-the-product** mindset. You graduate; the system stays. Runbooks, admin documentation, credential transfer, a maintenance guide, and a trained partner contact are not paperwork after the real work: for the partner, they largely *are* the product, and they are what the partner remembers once you are gone.

| Phase | What must be true |
|---|---|
| Early | IT/security review process mapped with dates; acceptance criteria written and partner-endorsed; prototype demoed from your hosting |
| Middle | Security review in progress or passed; staging instance in the partner's environment; feedback rounds addressed against the criteria |
| Late | Production deployment in partner environment; handoff package delivered; partner operating the system without you |

**Common blockers:** security review dead time (weeks with no visible progress), a single overloaded partner IT contact, credential and access provisioning, data that cannot leave the partner's network (design for this early), and partner staff turnover mid-year.

**Evidence to collect:** the signed acceptance criteria, meeting notes and emails showing feedback rounds and sign-offs, security review correspondence, screenshots of the system running in the partner's environment, the handoff package, and the partner's written statement of production use.

## Best Practices for Shipping

- Start every external clock in the first weeks: domain, TLS, hosting, store accounts, CLA, IRB, partner IT access. None of them runs faster for being started late.
- Deploy the walking skeleton while there is nothing to lose. A production URL with a placeholder page flushes out the whole dependency chain at zero risk.
- Instrument before you need the numbers. Analytics wired in the early phase is evidence by the late one; analytics wired a month before the end is a screenshot of an empty chart.
- Treat recruiting strangers as a workstream: an owner, a channel that exists, backlog items, starting no later than the middle phase.
- Put dates before the project's midpoint, not after it, and renegotiate the rung in the open when they slip rather than discovering the slip at the end.
- Read the ladder literally and plan for the rung you can prove, not the one you hope for.
- Capture evidence the day it happens (the merged PR, the dashboard, the maintainer's comment, the partner's email) and link it from `docs/shipped.md`.

## Some Truths About Shipping

Everything below follows from one fact stated at the top of this page: the long pole is other people, and other people do not respond to effort. That is what makes shipping feel different from building, where working harder reliably produces more.

The last tenth of a project is not a tenth of the work. A plan that says "build until a month before the end, then ship" is a plan for the bottom of the ladder, because the top rungs are gated on reviews, approvals, and users whose clocks that plan never started. The building estimate may even be right; it is the waiting that was never on the schedule.

Every external clock starts later than you think and runs longer than you were told. The estimate you get from an IT department, an IRB coordinator, or a maintainer is how long their step takes when nothing goes wrong. It leaves out the time your request sits in a queue before anyone reads it, and the revision round that follows. Apple's own figure is the pattern in miniature: most submissions are reviewed within a day, and that is the clock on one round, not on a rejection and a resubmission. Starting a clock before you need it is the only defense, because nothing you do later makes someone else's queue move faster.

A trivial thing shipped early beats an impressive thing shipped never. The walking skeleton, the typo pull request, and the sample-data pipeline have no value as features. Their value is that each one runs the whole external chain once (domain, TLS, contributor agreement, CI, review, data access) while failure is cheap, so the problems surface when you have months to fix them instead of days. None of them is the point, and all of them are what make the point reachable.

Strangers are not friends who have not heard of you yet. Friends try your software because they like you, and they forgive an onboarding flow that needs you on a call. Strangers owe you nothing, arrive slowly through channels that take weeks to build, and leave at the first confusing screen. That is why recruiting them is a workstream with an owner and backlog items, not a post you make the week you need them.

"The partner will deploy it after the project ends" is the feedback rung wearing the operations rung as a costume. Once the team is gone, nobody is left to fix the configuration that differs in the partner's environment, which is where deployments usually break, so a plan that stops short of deployment ends one rung lower than it claims. If it is not running in their environment before you leave, it has not shipped.

Maintainers, reviewers, IRB coordinators, and IT departments are people with queues, not APIs with service-level agreements. Each of them decides which of many requests to handle next, and a request from someone who was early, polite, and quick to answer the last question gets handled sooner. That makes courtesy a technical skill in the only sense that matters here: it changes how long the process takes.

If your shipping plan has no date before the project's midpoint, it is a wish rather than a plan. A late date cannot slip without taking the rung with it, while an early date that slips is information you can still act on, by renegotiating the rung in the open or by moving the work.

Evidence collected in the moment costs minutes. Evidence reconstructed at the end is thin, undated, and unconvincing, because the dashboard has been reset, the email thread is buried, and nobody remembers which week the first stranger signed up. Screenshot the milestone the day it happens.

## Shipping in Industry and Academia

Industry spent two decades removing the ceremony between a commit and production. [Continuous delivery](https://continuousdelivery.com/), [trunk-based development](https://trunkbaseddevelopment.com/), and [feature flags](https://martinfowler.com/articles/feature-toggles.html) exist so that "shipped" is a daily event rather than a quarterly one, and companies such as Etsy and Amazon made deploying many times a day the norm well before it was fashionable. The [DORA research program](https://dora.dev/) has spent a decade measuring which practices actually correlate with delivery performance, and deployment frequency is one of its four headline metrics. Google's site reliability teams run a [launch coordination checklist](https://sre.google/sre-book/launch-checklist/) before a service goes live, which is the top consultancy rung with a checklist attached. The pattern is the same at every scale: the thing that is hard is not building, it is the last mile into someone else's environment.

Open-source maintainers judge contributors by merged pull requests, and the first merge is the hard one, which is why "good first issue" labels and onboarding programs exist. In research, the reproducibility crisis produced [artifact evaluation](https://www.artifact-eval.org/): ACM conferences award badges for artifacts that are available, evaluated, and reproduced, and a paper with a reproduced artifact is worth more than one without. The [artifact evaluation process](https://ctuning.org/ae/) is now routine at systems and programming-language conferences, and the [Center for Open Science badges](https://www.cos.io/initiatives/badges) apply the same idea across other fields. The top research rung is written with that bar in mind.

The common thread is that every field has learned, expensively, that working software nobody can run is not a result. The ladder on this page is a small version of that lesson.

## Additional Readings

The clocks this page tells you to start are run by other people, and most of
them publish their own rules. Read the ones that apply to your project
early, because the cost of learning a requirement late is measured in weeks.
The remaining entries are the industry and research background for why the
ladder is written the way it is.

**Sources and further reading**

- [How to Contribute to Open Source](https://opensource.guide/how-to-contribute/), GitHub Open Source Guides: the contribution etiquette that decides whether your first pull request gets read.
- [Developer Certificate of Origin](https://developercertificate.org/): the one-paragraph alternative to a CLA that many projects use instead.
- [App Store Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/), Apple: the rules your submission is actually judged against.
- [Test tracks](https://developer.android.com/distribute/best-practices/launch/test-tracks), Android Developers: internal, closed, and open testing before production.
- [Continuous Delivery](https://continuousdelivery.com/), Jez Humble and David Farley: the book-length case for making release a non-event.
- [Trunk Based Development](https://trunkbaseddevelopment.com/), Paul Hammant: the branching model that makes daily deployment practical.
- [DORA](https://dora.dev/), Google Cloud: a decade of research on what actually predicts software delivery performance.
- [The SRE Book](https://sre.google/sre-book/table-of-contents/), Google: production readiness, launch coordination, and what operating a service really costs.
- [1,500 scientists lift the lid on reproducibility](https://www.nature.com/articles/533452a), Monya Baker, Nature: the survey behind the reproducibility crisis.
- [Artifact Evaluation](https://www.artifact-eval.org/): how computing conferences review the code behind a paper, and the badges they award.
- [Human Research Protection Program](https://research.oregonstate.edu/irb), Oregon State University: the IRB process, forms, and timelines for OSU projects.
