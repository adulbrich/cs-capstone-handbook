Sooner or later you'll stand in front of people who need something from your work: a sprint demo for the people who asked for the feature, an update for a stakeholder deciding whether the project is on track, a design review where your peers try to break the design before you build it. This page covers how to structure each of those talks, how to design slides that carry an argument, and how to run a live demo that survives the room. It doesn't cover writing documents, which [Documentation](/guides/documentation/) does, or how to run the sprint itself, which [Sprints](/guides/sprint/) does.

tl;dr:

- Decide the one thing the audience should leave knowing (a decision, a risk, or progress) and open with it.
- Demo working software on realistic data, on the machine and screen you'll present on, and record a fallback video before you walk in.
- Write each slide headline as a full-sentence claim and fill the body with the evidence for it (a chart, a diagram, a screenshot) instead of bullets.
- Say plainly what doesn't work yet; getting caught glossing over a gap costs far more than admitting it.
- End a demo on a question you want answered, not on a summary.
- Let the person who built each piece present it, and rehearse the handoffs between speakers.

Every section below serves one goal: leave the audience with an accurate understanding of what matters most, whether that's a decision they need to make, a risk they should know about, or progress they can rely on. Where two pieces of advice on this page conflict, the instruction team recommends following the one that serves that understanding, because it's the part of the talk the audience will act on after you've left the room.

Two teachers of technical communication are worth knowing by name. [Jean-luc Doumont](https://www.principiae.be/X0100.php) builds his book *Trees, Maps, and Theorems* on three laws: adapt to your audience, maximize the signal-to-noise ratio, and use effective redundancy. Patrick Winston's [How to Speak](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/res-tll-005-how-to-speak-january-iap-2018/), an MIT tradition for over 40 years and [recorded in full](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Unzc731iCUY), treats speaking as something you improve by learning a small set of heuristic rules rather than a talent you have or lack. If you watch one thing before your next talk, the instruction team recommends that hour, because each rule in it comes with an example of the talk it applies to: job talks, thesis defenses, oral exams, and lectures.

## Sprint Demos

A sprint demo is a live walkthrough of what the team delivered in the most recent sprint: working software, experimental outputs, or validated findings, shown running. A list of closed tickets is a status report, and the audience can read one on their own time; the demo is the one moment they see the work behave.

A good sprint demo:

- Shows the work end to end on realistic data, rather than describing what was attempted or what's planned next.
- Shows work that meets the team's Definition of Done. If "done" means reviewed, tested, and deployed to a staging environment, run the demo on staging. If the standard for this phase only requires passing tests locally, that's fine too; the demo should reflect whatever standard the team committed to.
- Connects what was built to a requirement or acceptance criterion the audience already knows.
- Says plainly what was descoped, delayed, or changed, and why.
- Ends with a question for the audience: "Does this meet your expectations? What should we change?"

The demo is the team's main feedback loop with the people it builds for, and it sits in the review step of the [sprint cycle](/guides/sprint/). Whoever is in the room, whether that's the person who asked for the software, a manager, or the rest of the team, the instruction team recommends running it as a conversation rather than a performance, because the feedback you need only arrives if the audience has room to give it. The difference shows in the last five minutes: a performance ends on a summary, and a conversation ends on a question you want answered.

## Stakeholder Presentations

A stakeholder presentation addresses a broader audience: people who care about the project's outcome but aren't involved in the day-to-day work. That includes leadership, management, cross-functional teams, the people who fund the work, and anyone evaluating the project from the outside.

Where a demo says "here's what we built this sprint", a stakeholder presentation says "here's where the project stands, how we got here, and where it's going." Effective ones tend to cover four things:

- The problem: what the project solves, and for whom. This grounds the audience before any technical content.
- The approach: how the team decided to solve it, and which alternatives it considered and rejected. Showing the rejected options is what tells the audience the choice was deliberate.
- The current state: what has been built, validated, or discovered so far. Show it: a working demo, a plot of real results, or a walkthrough of the running system convinces more than a summary of tasks completed.
- What comes next: the known limitations, the risks that remain, and the plan for the next phase.

At least one large company has decided that slides are the wrong medium for this altogether. In his [2017 letter to shareholders](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders), Jeff Bezos writes that Amazon doesn't use slide presentations in its meetings. Instead, someone writes a six-page narrative memo, everyone reads it silently at the start of the meeting in what he calls a kind of "study hall", and only then does the discussion begin. He adds that a great memo takes a week or more to write, rewritten and reviewed by colleagues. You'll almost certainly be asked for slides rather than a memo, but the instruction team recommends borrowing the habit anyway: write the argument as paragraphs first, check that it holds together, and only then decide which parts need a slide, because prose won't let you skip the connection between two claims the way a bullet list will.

## Design Reviews

A design review is a presentation to peers (teammates, advisors, anyone qualified to critique the approach) before the team commits to it. It applies to more than architecture and code: UI designs, data models, research methods, and workflow proposals all benefit from structured critique while changing them is still cheap.

The purpose is to stress-test the design, and that works best with a written [technical design](/guides/technical-design/) to react to rather than a verbal sketch. Present the problem, the options you considered, the one you chose, and its consequences, then ask for objections. A problem found in the review costs a conversation; the same problem found during implementation costs the code already built on top of it.

Treat hard questions as help. If a review produces no changes at all, ask whether the design is really that mature or whether nobody in the room felt able to object, and next time ask one reviewer in advance to argue for the option you rejected.

## Public Demonstrations

Some presentations happen in open, public settings: expos, showcases, poster sessions, or employer events. These work differently from a team-internal or classroom presentation, because the audience chooses itself, arrives in the middle of your explanation, and leaves whenever it wants.

What changes:

- The demo leads. Have it running before the doors open, looping or ready to restart, and show visitors the thing working before you explain it. Don't rely on a single run-through.
- The audience is mixed. Some visitors are deeply technical and many aren't. You need an explanation that works for both, and the running software is the part both understand.
- You don't control the timing. Someone may walk up at any moment, so be ready to start cold.
- Brevity is the fallback, not the plan. Many visitors will only give you a minute, so keep a 60-second pitch ready for the one who won't stop walking. It comes after the demo, not instead of it.

## Principles That Apply to Every Presentation

The habits below hold whatever the format, from a five-minute sprint demo to a conference talk.

### Lead with the Point

Don't build to a conclusion; start with it. "This sprint we shipped user authentication and onboarded our first external tester" is a stronger opening than three slides of context, because the audience knows what to listen for in everything that follows.

The rule applies at every level: the presentation as a whole, each section, each slide. If someone walked in halfway through and saw only the current slide, would they understand the point it makes?

### Show, Don't Tell

If you can show something working, show it rather than describing it. A running feature answers questions a slide about it can't, such as how fast it responds and what it does with bad input. Reserve slides for what you can't demo: architecture diagrams, risk tables, timelines, before-and-after comparisons, data visualizations.

For research-oriented projects, showing means displaying real outputs: plots, model performance metrics, pipeline results on actual data. A Jupyter notebook with real outputs convinces more than a slide summarizing what the notebook produced, because the audience can see the numbers came from code that ran.

### Design for Your Audience

Technical presentations to peers can include architecture diagrams, code snippets, and implementation detail. Stakeholder presentations usually shouldn't. Before you keep a slide, ask whether a non-engineer in this audience would understand it.

The same project often needs both versions. A sprint demo for the people who asked for the software focuses on functionality and outcomes; a design review with the team focuses on architecture and trade-offs. Adjust the depth and the vocabulary to whoever is listening.

### Let Ownership Guide Who Speaks

The person closest to a piece of work should be the one who explains it, in sprint demos, stakeholder presentations, and design reviews alike. The instruction team recommends dividing the talk by ownership (the person who built the feature explains the feature, the person who ran the experiment presents the results) rather than splitting it evenly by slide count, because the owner can answer the follow-up question without handing it back to a teammate.

### Prepare for Questions

Spend a real share of your preparation anticipating questions and rehearsing the answers. The hard questions in technical presentations are predictable:

- "Why did you choose X instead of Y?"
- "What happens if this component fails?"
- "How does this scale beyond your current usage?"
- "What's the biggest risk right now?"

You don't need perfect answers. "We considered that and chose not to, because..." and "We don't know yet, and here's how we plan to find out" are both credible. What damages trust is a question that shows nobody on the team thought about it at all.

## Delivering a Demo

A live demo can fail in more ways than a slide deck can (the network, an expired account, a database someone reset), so most of the work happens before you're in the room.

- Use realistic data. Obviously fake data ("Test User 1", "Lorem ipsum", "AAAA") makes the audience wonder what else isn't real. Populate the demo environment with data that looks like the real thing.
- Know the path. Practice the exact sequence of interactions: what you'll click, what you'll type, and what the system will show in response.
- Have a fallback. Record a video of the demo before the presentation. If the live demo breaks, play the recording and keep talking; the audience still sees the feature work.
- Narrate as you go. Don't click through screens in silence. Say what you're doing and why: "I'm logging in as an admin and opening the project dashboard, where you can see the new analytics view."
- Leave the demo visible. After the walkthrough, keep the application or notebook open rather than switching back to slides, so it's there for follow-up questions.

## Recorded vs. Live Presentations

Each format needs different preparation.

For recorded presentations:

- Use a quiet room with consistent lighting.
- Record the screen and a face camera together; seeing the speaker makes a recording easier to follow. [OBS Studio](https://obsproject.com/) is free and cross-platform, and Loom or Zoom's local recording are quicker to set up if you only need one take.
- Edit out dead air and false starts. A viewer can skip ahead, but every skip is a moment they stopped listening.
- Speak slightly slower than feels natural. The instruction team recommends it because nerves speed most speakers up, and a recording can't slow down for a confused viewer the way you would in the room.

For live presentations:

- Practice the full run-through, including the handoffs between speakers, at least once on the setup you'll use. The [TEDx organizer guide](https://www.ted.com/tedx/organizer-guide/prepare-speakers-and-program) goes much further: its speakers rehearse for weeks or months and attend at least one dress rehearsal onstage with their slides.
- Confirm the technical setup (display resolution, audio, browser, microphone) on the actual machine and screen you'll use.
- Arrive early. A problem found five minutes before you start is an emergency; the same problem found thirty minutes before is an inconvenience.

## Slide Design

You don't need design expertise to make effective slides, but you do need a position on what a slide is for. PowerPoint's default layout, a topic phrase over a box of bullets, is the most common one: estimates in the studies <Cite id="garner-2013" /> review range from 65% to 80% of slides. That layout puts on the screen the same words that are coming out of your mouth, and multimedia-learning research finds that words shown and spoken at the same time compete for the same working memory <Cite id="garner-2013" form="parenthetical" />. The audience ends up choosing between reading and listening.

The alternative is the assertion-evidence structure, developed by Michael Alley at Penn State and documented, with templates, at [assertion-evidence.com](https://www.assertion-evidence.com/). Each slide gets a headline that states a complete sentence rather than a topic ("Latency doubles above 200 concurrent users", not "Performance"), and the body of the slide is the visual evidence for that sentence: a chart, a diagram, a screenshot. The bullets disappear because the headline carries the claim and the image carries the support. It takes longer to build, and it exposes a vague slide immediately, because a sentence headline has to say something.

It's also one of the few pieces of slide advice that has been tested. Garner and Alley randomly assigned 110 engineering undergraduates to watch the same narrated presentation on MRI, with either assertion-evidence slides or slides built on PowerPoint's defaults. The assertion-evidence group wrote essays that showed better comprehension and fewer misconceptions, rated the talk as less mental effort, and scored higher on an unannounced recall test given later. On the immediate multiple-choice test the two groups did about equally well on facts; the difference showed on the questions that required inference <Cite id="garner-2013" form="parenthetical" />. That's one study, on one topic, with students as the audience, so treat the size of the effect as uncertain; the direction is the reason to try the structure.

With that settled, the rest is mechanical:

- One idea per slide. If a slide makes two separate points, split it. A headline that needs the word "and" is usually two slides.
- Diagrams over prose. A system architecture diagram, a data-flow visualization, or an experimental workflow communicates more than three paragraphs of description, and the audience can look at it while you talk.
- Readable type. The instruction team recommends at least 24pt for body text, because a slide that's readable on your laptop is often unreadable from the back of a room. Check contrast rather than trusting your monitor: the [WCAG contrast minimum](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html) of 4.5:1 for body text is the threshold to design against, and a projector in a bright room washes out colors further than any screen you tested on.
- Attribute borrowed assets. If you use an image, icon, or diagram you didn't create, include a source credit, for the same reason you'd cite a source in writing.

## Best Practices

- Practice the full presentation at least once before delivering it, especially the demo.
- Time yourself. Running well over your slot tells the audience you didn't rehearse, and it takes the time from whoever speaks next.
- Get feedback from someone outside the team before the final delivery. They'll catch the jargon and skipped steps that the team no longer notices.
- Don't apologize for things being "in progress." State the current state plainly and explain what comes next.
- Know your handoffs. "I'll now hand it over to Sam, who will walk through the data pipeline" is smoother than trailing off while someone else starts unexpectedly.

## Some Truths About Presentations

Most presentation advice, including some of the advice on this page, is asserted rather than tested. The assertion-evidence structure has a controlled comparison behind it <Cite id="garner-2013" form="parenthetical" />; "make eye contact" and "never read your slides" are craft advice that happens to be widely agreed on. Both can be useful, but they're different kinds of claim. On this page, a finding carries a citation, and a sentence that begins "The instruction team recommends" is the judgment of people who have watched a lot of demos, with its reason attached so you can weigh it.

What an audience likes and what it learns from can come apart. In Garner and Alley's study, the students who saw bullet-heavy slides rated the amount of text on them as more appropriate than the assertion-evidence group rated theirs, while learning less from them <Cite id="garner-2013" form="parenthetical" />. "I liked the slides" tells you the audience was comfortable, so when you want to know whether a talk worked, ask the audience what they'd do next or how they'd explain the result to someone else.

Sprint demos on a student project are a partial simulation, and everyone in the room knows it. In industry the audience controls a budget and can cancel the feature; on a student project the audience is judging how you work and has already committed to the project continuing. That changes the incentives: the full disclosure that could cost you a feature at work costs you very little here, which makes the demo a cheap place to practice a habit that's expensive to learn later. Use it that way rather than treating the demo as a performance review.

Rehearsal is easy to skip, because it's boring and tweaking slides feels like work. The failures it prevents are specific: demos break on the machine, the adapter, the resolution, the network, and the one account whose password nobody remembers. A rehearsal on your own laptop catches almost none of those. A rehearsal on the actual machine, projected onto the actual screen, catches most of them, which is why the recommendation above is narrower than "practice more" and why the recorded fallback exists.

The instruction team recommends disclosing gaps by default, even ones you're fairly sure nobody would notice, because the costs are lopsided. "This part doesn't work yet, and here's why" costs little and shows you know where the edges of your system are. Glossing over a gap and getting caught costs much more: once one claim turns out to be wrong, the audience has a reason to doubt every other claim in the talk.

Being a strong engineer doesn't make you a strong presenter, and pretending otherwise helps nobody. Winston's lecture rests on the premise that speaking improves when you learn specific rules and practice them. If presenting is hard for you, that tells you where to spend practice time, not whether you belong in the field.

## Presentations in Industry and Academia

In industry, engineers present constantly: sprint demos, design reviews, incident postmortems, all-hands updates, sales engineering calls, and cross-team coordination meetings. Companies write the skill into their expectations. Dropbox's published [engineering career framework](https://dropbox.github.io/dbx-career-framework/ic5_staff_software_engineer.html) lists Communication among the expectations for its entry-level engineers (IC1) and its staff engineers (IC5), and at staff level it expects an engineer to tailor the message to the audience and present it "at the right altitude".

New graduates tend to find this harder than the technical work. <Cite id="begel-2008" /> observed eight recent graduates for 85 hours during their first six months as developers at Microsoft and sorted the ways they got stuck into five kinds (communication, collaboration, technical, cognition, and orientation), only one of them technical. On communication, the new developers sometimes went into too much detail in design meetings, at other times gave too little, and didn't push for enough detail in the answers they got back, which is the "design for your audience" problem above, observed in the field.

In academia, conference talks and thesis defenses follow the same principles. A poster session has more in common with a public demo at an engineering expo than either audience might expect: short attention, mixed expertise, and a presenter who adjusts depth to whoever is asking. The assertion-evidence research itself came out of engineering education (Garner and Alley ran their study with students from a communications course for engineers), and Doumont's and Winston's teaching is aimed at researchers and academics who present for a living.

The two worlds differ most in what counts as evidence. An industry stakeholder wants to see the thing working; a conference audience wants to see the method and the numbers that let them judge whether it generalizes. A student project shown at a public showcase usually needs both, because the people walking past include employers who want a demo and faculty who want a method.
