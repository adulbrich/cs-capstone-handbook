Your team has just formed, or it has worked together long enough that a habit nobody chose is starting to grate: pull requests that sit unreviewed for a week, a decision made in a private message, a teammate who goes quiet from Friday to Monday. This page covers the working agreement (sometimes called a team charter): what it settles, how to write one together, and how to keep it current. It doesn't cover how to confront a teammate who isn't contributing, which is the [conflict guide](/guides/conflict/)'s subject, or the branch and review rules for the code, which belong in `CONTRIBUTING.md`.

:::note[tl;dr]
- Write the agreement together, in one 45-to-60-minute session with everyone present.
- Give each concern exactly one owner: the schedule and partner relationship, the shared AI configuration and confidentiality rule, and the Definition of Done and state of the build.
- Write every norm with a trigger someone can observe ("acknowledge within 24 hours on weekdays"), never an attitude ("be responsive").
- Decide in advance which decisions one person makes, which the team makes, which go to your project partner, and what silence on a proposal means.
- Keep it with the team's own documents rather than in the code repository, short enough to read in five minutes, a few pages at most.
- Revise it after your first retrospective, after a significant conflict, when the scope changes, and at each new phase of the project.
:::

A working agreement is a document the team writes together to make its operating model explicit: how decisions get made, how people communicate, what "done" means, and what happens when something isn't working. Every team develops those habits anyway. The agreement decides whether they form on purpose or by accident, and it gives the team something to point at when one person's expectation and another's habit disagree.

## Why Working Agreements Matter

Write the agreement before the first technical argument, because a team of strong developers can still stall on process: which branching strategy to use, who breaks a tie, what "finished" means. Those questions are cheaper to settle in the abstract, before anyone's code or pride depends on the answer.

Google's [Project Aristotle](https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/understand-team-effectiveness) is the best-known attempt to measure which of these actually matters; it's an internal study of 180 Google teams, published on Google's re:Work site rather than in a journal. Studying its own teams, Google found that who was on the team mattered less than how the team operated, and that the strongest single predictor was psychological safety: whether members believed they could take an interpersonal risk, such as admitting a mistake or asking an obvious question, without being penalized for it. Use the agreement to build psychological safety: decide in it what happens when someone says they're stuck, so the person saying it knows the response before they speak.

<Cite id="edmondson-1999" /> defined team psychological safety and, in a study of 51 manufacturing teams, found it associated with the team's learning behavior. She built that study on her earlier work with hospital patient-care teams, where members of some teams openly acknowledged a medication error and discussed how to avoid a repeat, while members of others kept it to themselves <Cite id="edmondson-1999" form="parenthetical" />. The hospital study found that the teams differed not only in how often errors happened but in how likely an error was to be detected and learned from <Cite id="edmondson-1996" form="parenthetical" />.

<Cite id="aaron-2014" /> compared business-student teams under several charter conditions and found that introducing a charter improved communication, effort, mutual support, cohesion, and member satisfaction. <Cite id="mathieu-2009" /> followed 32 MBA teams through a business simulation: the quality of a team's performance strategy, its plan for the task, predicted its performance, and the teams that sustained the highest performance had both a high-quality charter and a high-quality strategy. Write the agreement alongside your project plan, then, rather than in place of one.

The need is greatest on a team that formed quickly, whose members don't know each other well, and that has no manager to set norms. That team defines its own operating model, or one emerges by default.

## What Effective Agreements Cover

No single template fits every team, but effective agreements settle the same few areas. Treat them as conversations to have early rather than sections to fill in.

### Roles and Ownership

Make sure everyone knows who to go to for what, because a question with no obvious owner goes to whoever answers first, or to nobody. This doesn't mean rigid hierarchy. It means clarity, and the useful unit is the concern rather than the job title. Three concerns need an owner on almost any team: the schedule and the partner relationship, the shared AI configuration and the confidentiality rule, and the Definition of Done and the state of the build. Call the owners a Project Manager, an AI Coordinator, and a Quality Owner if you want labels, and name them once rather than letting a second informal set grow alongside.

Count owners per concern, not per person. A concern with two owners has none, because each will assume the other is handling it. A team of two has one person holding two concerns and should say which; a larger team has people holding none of the three, who should own something else nameable instead: a subsystem, the test suite, the deployment. Coordination work the three don't cover, such as keeping documentation current and facilitating stand-ups and retrospectives, is what those additional areas are for, and assigning it explicitly stops it falling on whoever notices first.

### Decision-Making

Not every decision needs a team meeting. A useful agreement distinguishes three levels:

- **Individual decisions**: implementation details within your own work.
- **Team decisions**: anything that affects others' work, the architecture, or the project's direction. Discuss these (in a stand-up, the team channel, or a dedicated meeting) before acting.
- **Escalated decisions**: blockers, pivots, or changes that affect the project partner. These involve the partner, and whoever supervises the team.

A default rule for silence keeps team decisions moving. The Apache Software Foundation calls it [lazy consensus](https://community.apache.org/committers/decisionMaking.html): state your intent in the shared channel, wait a set time for objections (Apache usually waits 72 hours), and treat silence as consent. A 24-hour window suits a small team that talks daily, for low-stakes, easy-to-reverse choices; a hard-to-reverse decision deserves an explicit yes from everyone it affects.

### Communication

Be specific about how the team stays in touch:

- Where conversations happen. Pick one primary channel (Slack, Discord, Teams) and commit to it.
- How fast a message gets answered. "Acknowledge messages within 24 hours on weekdays" is a norm; "respond promptly" isn't.
- When the team meets: stand-ups, sprint reviews, and retrospectives, each with a day and a time.
- How someone flags something urgent.

Choose the channel your team already opens every day. A response-time norm only works on messages people see, and each extra channel is one more place a message can sit unread.

### Definition of Done

The Definition of Done (DoD) answers one question: *what does it mean for a task to be finished?* It comes from Scrum. The [Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html) makes it the commitment attached to the increment, "a formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required for the product", and says that an item that doesn't meet it can't be released or even presented at the sprint review.

Without a shared DoD, "done" means different things to different people. One person considers a feature done when it works on their machine. Another considers it done when it's deployed, reviewed, and documented. The mismatch surfaces at integration time and during sprint reviews, when the gap is most expensive to close.

A DoD doesn't need to be elaborate, but it does need to be written down and agreed on. It usually grows as the project matures: early sprints might require only a reviewed PR and passing tests, while later sprints add deployment and documentation. The team agrees on it together, as part of the working agreement, and writes it down in `CONTRIBUTING.md`, because the people and AI tools that merge code read that file and never see the agreement.

### Conflict Resolution

Every team disagrees. Define how the team handles it before tensions arise, while nobody is angry yet. A simple escalation path:

1. A direct conversation between the people involved.
2. A team discussion, in a retrospective or a dedicated meeting.
3. Escalation to someone outside the team with authority over it, such as a manager or an advisor.

Write escalation into the agreement as an ordinary step, because a team that treats it as a failure tends to wait until the problem can no longer be fixed. The [conflict guide](/guides/conflict/) owns the performance conversation and the escalation path in detail; what the agreement contributes is the part decided in advance.

### Inclusion

Agree explicitly on how the team makes sure every member is heard, especially in design discussions, code reviews, and retrospectives. Practical approaches include:

- Rotating who facilitates meetings.
- Posting proposals in writing before a decision, so everyone has time to think.
- Inviting quieter members to give their view before closing a discussion.

These are the concrete form of the psychological safety finding above. A rotating facilitator and an explicit invitation to speak are mechanisms anyone can check, where "we value everyone's input" is a sentiment nobody can. Check at each retrospective whether they're still happening, because nothing breaks visibly when they lapse.

### AI Tool Usage

Your team will probably use AI coding assistants: in the <Cite id="stackoverflow-2025" /> developer survey, an industry report with a self-selected sample, 84% of the roughly 34,000 who answered the question said they use or plan to use AI tools in their development process.

Settle three questions early, because teammates who each prompt their own assistant their own way can leave the codebase with inconsistent patterns, conflicting abstractions, and code nobody on the team fully understands:

- Which tools and configurations does the team share? If the team uses a tool that supports project-level instructions (such as `CLAUDE.md`, agent skills, or a shared system prompt), agree on a single configuration checked into the repository, so every teammate's assistant gets the same architectural context.
- What review does AI-generated code get? A reasonable default is the same review as human-written code. The author is responsible for understanding and defending what they commit, whoever (or whatever) wrote the first draft.
- How does the team handle an architectural change an AI tool suggests? Treat it like any design change: discuss it, check it against the design document, and write an ADR if the team adopts it. Nobody merges a structural change just because an assistant proposed it.

These norms apply differently depending on the phase of work. During early exploration, prototypes, and spikes, let the team experiment loosely with AI tools: a prototype exists to learn fast and be thrown away, and requiring full design compliance and rigorous review for throwaway code slows down the experiments that lead to better design decisions. The norms above matter most for code meant to last: anything merged into the main branch, deployed, or built on by others.

These norms belong in the working agreement because they're about how the team coordinates rather than about the tools. The technical side (which architectural constraints to encode, how to keep AI output consistent with the design) is in the technical design guide.

## From Agreement to Repository

A working agreement captures *how the team operates*. A `CONTRIBUTING.md` file in your repository captures *how someone contributes to the codebase*. The two are related but serve different readers.

Your working agreement is for the team. It covers roles, communication, conflict resolution, and norms. Your `CONTRIBUTING.md` is for anyone who touches the code: current teammates, future contributors, and reviewers. It covers branching strategy, PR requirements, code style, testing expectations, the Definition of Done, and the contribution floor: what each person lands per sprint and where each kind of work lives.

Think of `CONTRIBUTING.md` as the technical expression of your working agreement. When the agreement says "all work goes through reviewed pull requests," `CONTRIBUTING.md` says what that means in practice: branch naming, review turnaround, merge strategy.

:::tip[Starting Points]
You don't need to write your `CONTRIBUTING.md` from scratch. GitHub has a [guide to CONTRIBUTING.md](https://docs.github.com/en/communities/setting-up-your-project-for-healthy-contributions/setting-guidelines-for-repository-contributors), and large open-source projects publish theirs: [Node.js](https://github.com/nodejs/node/blob/main/CONTRIBUTING.md) and [Ruby on Rails](https://guides.rubyonrails.org/contributing_to_ruby_on_rails.html) are both worth reading for how much they spell out.
:::

## Writing Your Agreement

Write the agreement together. When one person drafts it and the rest sign off, the others have agreed to read it rather than to the norms in it, and the disagreements the drafting was meant to surface stay hidden until they cost something.

A short working session (45 to 60 minutes) is usually enough:

1. Each person writes their top three non-negotiables for working well in a team.
2. Share and discuss them. Look for consensus and surface tensions early.
3. Draft the document together during the session.
4. Review the communication cadence and the Definition of Done with your project partner.

Atlassian's [working agreements play](https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/working-agreements) is a facilitated version of the same session, if your team wants a script.

Before the session, name the stage your team is in, and spell out more (explicit roles, response times) the less the team knows each other, because members who haven't worked together can't fall back on habits they don't share yet. <Cite id="tuckman-1965" /> reviewed 50 studies of group development and proposed four stages; on the interpersonal side, a group moves from testing and dependence, through conflict and then cohesion, to functional roles. The [Team Formation Strategies Assessment](/activities/teamwork/#team-formation-strategies-assessment) walks those stages under their popular labels, forming to performing.

Keep the agreement with the team's own documents, such as a shared drive or the team wiki, not in the code repository. It is an agreement between the people on the team, while the repository is read by the project partner and inherited by whoever maintains the code next.

Kept to the conversations above, the agreement stays short:

```md
# Team Charter

## Roles (rotate at each phase review)
- Project Manager, Sam: board, sprint planning, partner liaison.
- AI Coordinator, Priya: AGENTS.md, skills, the confidentiality rule.
- Quality Owner, Jordan: CI, test suite, the Definition of Done.

## Decisions
- Individual: your own task. Team: architecture, dependencies, scope,
  proposed in the channel; silence for 24 h is a yes.
- Escalated: partner-facing changes, blockers over one sprint.

## Communication
- Discord #shift-board. Acknowledge within 24 h on weekdays.
- Stand-up Tue and Thu 17:00. Review and retro every other Friday.
- Availability: 9 h/week each; interview weeks announced a sprint ahead.

## Merging
Definition of Done and the contribution floor: CONTRIBUTING.md.

## Inclusion
Facilitator rotates each meeting; anyone who hasn't spoken is asked
before a decision closes. Accommodation requests go privately to the PM.

## When it isn't working
Talk directly within a week; raise it at the next retro; after two
sprints with no change, escalate to our supervisor.

## AI and confidentiality
Shared config in AGENTS.md; generated code is reviewed like any other.
Any tool: our code, docs, synthetic data. Partner-approved tools only:
partner API docs. Never: production data, personal data, credentials.
```

Keep yours short enough to read in five minutes, a few pages at most, because the agreement gets consulted in the middle of a disagreement, and nobody reads ten pages to find the clause they need.

## Keeping It Alive

An agreement written in the first weeks and never reopened stops describing the team. A few months later the project has changed, the dynamics have shifted, and some norms have proven unrealistic. <Cite id="luvison-2025" /> found that student teams who revised their charter midway through the project showed significantly higher team commitment, team satisfaction, and individual effectiveness than teams who wrote a charter once when they formed. The revised-charter teams also received feedback and coaching, so the effect belongs to the three together.

Revisit it at the natural moments:

- At the start of each new phase of the project: does it still reflect how the team actually works?
- After a significant conflict: what process gap allowed it?
- When the project scope changes: do the roles and the DoD still apply?

Keep the agreement's earlier versions, which the version history of a shared document does for you. Its history shows how the team's norms changed and when, which is useful to the team and to anyone who later needs to understand how it operated.

## When Things Go Wrong

Use the agreement against slow erosion more than against a blowup: a teammate who misses deadlines, posts vague updates, and leaves tasks in progress for a sprint with nothing to show. A blowup forces a conversation on its own; erosion never produces that moment, so the team needs something to point at. The agreement's contribution is to make that conversation concrete. When it states availability in hours, says that a blocker is raised at the next stand-up rather than sat on for a week, and the team has a minimum deliverable per person per sprint that the board can show, then "I feel like you're not contributing" becomes "the board shows no completed tasks from you in the last two sprints", which is observable and hard to deflect.

How to have that conversation, when a bad week becomes a pattern, and when to escalate are the [conflict guide](/guides/conflict/#performance-conversations)'s subject.

### When a Role Goes Unfilled

Assigning someone a role, such as documentation owner or facilitator, doesn't guarantee they'll do it. If the person responsible for a role consistently neglects it, the team has two choices:

1. Reassign the role to someone willing to do it, and have a direct conversation about why.
2. Redistribute the work so the role is shared rather than owned by one person.

Quietly picking up someone else's responsibilities without addressing the gap doesn't work. The person covering builds resentment, and the rest of the team learns that an assignment in the agreement can be ignored without consequence.

## Validation

Check the agreement against how the team behaves, not against who signed it:

- Every teammate can say, without looking, where decisions get made and how fast a message gets answered. If the answers differ, the agreement was written but never agreed.
- Your project partner has seen the communication cadence and the Definition of Done and didn't object.
- The first retrospective checked it against what actually happened and changed at least one line. A first draft rarely matches a real sprint, so an untouched agreement usually means nobody compared the two.

## Measuring Success

The agreement is working when it changes behavior, which is harder to see than whether it exists. Each signal below can be checked without asking anyone how the team feels.

- **Time to raise**: how long between a norm being broken and someone saying so. One sprint is good; a whole phase of the project is the failure mode.
- **Where decisions happen**: a design change that first surfaced in a pull request rather than the channel shows the decision levels aren't being used.
- **Rotation on schedule**: roles changed hands when the agreement said they would, with a written record of who chaired what.

## Best Practices

- Be specific. "Acknowledge within 24 hours on weekdays" is a norm; "respond promptly" isn't, because nobody can say when it was broken, so enforcing it means arguing about a teammate's attitude.
- Separate team norms from project standards. Conflict resolution belongs in the working agreement; branching strategy belongs in `CONTRIBUTING.md`.
- Include a process for changing it. "Any teammate can propose a change; the team discusses it at the next retrospective" is a reasonable default.
- Write it in a live session, in person or on a call, because a comment thread lets people skip the disagreement a conversation forces into the open.

## Some Truths About Working Agreements

A working agreement written because someone required one tends to be written once, in the first weeks, and never opened again. Everyone involved can tell this is happening and nobody says so, because the document looks complete and the deadline has passed. Put yours where the team already looks, linked from the board and opened at every retrospective, so re-reading it is part of a meeting the team already holds.

A working agreement can't fix a team that has a real problem, and it's sometimes used to avoid having one. Writing "we will communicate respectfully" is easier than telling a teammate their code review comments are hostile, and a team can spend a productive-feeling hour drafting norms to avoid the five-minute conversation it actually needs. Test each clause by asking whether it would change what a specific person does next week, and if none would, have the conversation the drafting was standing in for.

Expect parts of the agreement to be wrong within a few months. The team that wrote it didn't know how much time the project would take, which tools would stick, or who would turn out to be reliable at what. When a clause is broken often, treat that as a sign the clause may be unrealistic before concluding the team is failing, and use the retrospective to find out which.

## Working Agreements in Industry and Academia

In industry, agile teams write working agreements under many names: team charter, team manifesto, team norms, social contract. Atlassian's Team Playbook, linked above, publishes a format for writing one. Large engineering organizations also document norms that apply across every team, such as coding standards, on-call expectations, and incident response; Google's [engineering practices](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/), which set out its code review standards, are a public example.

GitLab's [all-remote handbook](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/company/culture/all-remote/) and Basecamp's [How We Communicate](https://basecamp.com/guides/how-we-communicate) show what a company-scale working agreement looks like in public, and both are far longer than yours needs to be. Read them for the kind of decision they bother to write down, which is defaults rather than values: which channel is for what, how long a reply may take, what's a meeting and what's a document.

A company that writes its norms down can hand them to a new hire on the first day instead of leaving them to be learned from mistakes. Your `CONTRIBUTING.md` does the same for anyone who joins your project or reviews your work.

In academia, the charter studies cited on this page all used student teams: business students <Cite id="aaron-2014" form="parenthetical" />, MBA students <Cite id="mathieu-2009" form="parenthetical" />, and student project teams <Cite id="luvison-2025" form="parenthetical" />.

For a student team, the agreement is also a record of how the team meant to operate, which gives the team, and whoever supervises it, a starting point when something stops working.
