Your team has a partner, a project idea, and notes from a first meeting where everyone seemed to agree on what to build. A week later two teammates are building different versions of the same feature, and the partner brings up something new at every meeting. This page covers how to write requirements a teammate can test, what goes in `docs/requirements.md`, and how to keep that file accurate as the project changes. It doesn't cover technology and architecture choices, which belong in an [ADR](/guides/adr/) and your [technical design](/guides/technical-design/), or how to order the work within a sprint, which the [planning guide](/guides/planning/) covers.

:::note[tl;dr]
- Open `docs/requirements.md` with a one-paragraph problem statement: what the problem is, who has it, and what evidence you have that it's real.
- Write each requirement so a teammate could mark it passed or failed without asking you, and give every quality requirement a number and a way to measure it.
- Sort requirements into functional, non-functional, and constraints, give each an ID (FR-1, NFR-1, C-1), and rank them with a reason.
- Write down the non-goals, and give every open question an owner.
- Mark which requirements your partner or a user has confirmed and which are still assumptions.
- Change the file in the same pull request as the code that changes the behavior.
:::

A requirement is a specific, testable statement about what the product must do or how well it must do it. It isn't a wish ("the app should be fast"), a task ("build the login page"), or a design decision ("use PostgreSQL"). Tasks go on the board and design decisions go in an ADR. Keeping requirements apart from solutions is harder than it sounds: <Cite id="mendezfernandez-2017" /> surveyed 228 companies in 10 countries for the Naming the Pain in Requirements Engineering (NaPiRE) study, and a quarter of the respondents ranked stakeholders' difficulty separating requirements from known solution designs among their five most critical requirements problems.

Keep the requirements in the repository as `docs/requirements.md`, next to the code rather than in a shared drive. There, a pull request can change a requirement in the same commit as the behavior it describes, and your AI coding tools can read the file before they build anything; [AI project setup](/guides/ai-project-setup/) shows how to point them at it.

## What Is a Requirement?

Sort requirements into three kinds, and give each kind its own section in the document:

- **Functional**: what the system does. "A user can reset their password by email." "The pipeline rejects a record whose timestamp is missing."
- **Non-functional**: how well it does it, with a number. "The dashboard loads in under 3 seconds on the partner's office Wi-Fi." "A fresh clone runs the full pipeline in one documented command."
- **Constraints**: limits you didn't choose. "Must run on the partner's existing Postgres 14." "No personal data leaves the partner's network." "Ships under the upstream project's license." An inherited codebase belongs here too, because its CI, its review rules, and its hosting bind you before you write a line.

A longer write-up of what you inherited, covering how it builds, how it's tested, and how a change gets reviewed, can sit under the Constraints heading too. [Analyze an Existing Codebase](/activities/reflective/#analyze-an-existing-codebase) is the exercise that produces one.

Textbooks divide requirements further: Wiegers and Beatty's [*Software Requirements*](https://www.microsoftpressstore.com/store/software-requirements-9780735679665), for example, gives business requirements and user requirements a chapter each. Those categories answer whose requirement it is, which makes them good questions to ask in an interview and poor headings for a document, since a reader looking for a requirement searches by what it's about.

The formal treatment is [ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/29148/6937/), the international standard for requirements engineering of systems and software. It defines the construct of a good requirement, the attributes and characteristics a requirement carries, and the information items a requirements process produces. Read its overview once to see what full rigor involves, and adopt it when a contract or a regulator asks you to; short of that, the time its formality costs is better spent confirming requirements with users.

Requirements come from the people who have the problem: your partner, the people who will use the product, the maintainers of the project you're joining. Start with interviews and a few observed sessions of the current workflow, then look at what existing tools already do. In an interview, ask what people did the last time the problem came up rather than what they would want, because a question about the future gets an opinion and a question about the past gets a fact. Rob Fitzpatrick makes that argument at book length in [The Mom Test](https://www.momtestbook.com/).

## Addressing a Problem

Start from a problem that a group of people you can name actually has. A clear problem gives every requirement a reason to exist, and it gives you a way to decline the requests that don't serve it: a feature that touches nothing in the problem statement is a different project, and you can say so.

Open the document with a **problem statement**: what the problem is, who has it, why it matters, and what evidence you have that it's real, in one paragraph. Expect to rewrite it. The first version is written before the team has watched anyone work, and each interview or prototype can show that the problem sits somewhere else. When you reframe it, say what changed in the commit message, so the file's history records what the team learned and when.

## What Makes a Good Requirement?

Write each requirement so a teammate could mark it passed or failed without asking you. That takes specific nouns, a measurable bar for anything about quality, and an acceptance criterion wherever the behavior isn't obvious. "The system should be fast" can't be checked, so it can't be finished either; "the dashboard loads in under three seconds on the partner's office Wi-Fi" can be both. The difference shows up at the demo, where an untestable requirement turns into an argument about whether it was met.

A [user story](https://www.mountaingoatsoftware.com/agile/user-stories) states a functional requirement from the user's side: "As a coordinator, I want to see open shifts, so that I can fill gaps before the weekend." Mike Cohn's Mountain Goat Software argues that the written story is a reminder to have a conversation, and that the full requirement comes out of that conversation.

<Cite id="lucassen-2016" /> surveyed 182 practitioners: 59% used the template "As a role, I want goal, [so that benefit]", in which the "so that" clause is optional, and respondents reported that stories, a template, and quality guidelines improved their productivity and the quality of their work. Interviewees in the same study nonetheless called the "so that" clause essential, and credited it with less confusion among stakeholders, less discussion, and more autonomy for developers <Cite id="lucassen-2016" form="parenthetical" />.

Bill Wake's [INVEST](https://xp123.com/invest-in-good-stories-and-smart-tasks/) mnemonic checks a single story: is it Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, and Testable? It's the best-known set of guidelines for user stories, though only 23.5% of the practitioners in the same survey used it, while 39.5% used no guidelines at all <Cite id="lucassen-2016" form="parenthetical" />.

For acceptance criteria, [Given-When-Then](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/GivenWhenThen.html) states a precondition, an action, and the expected result. [Gherkin](https://cucumber.io/docs/gherkin/reference/) formalizes that pattern into executable specifications that Cucumber runs as tests, so a criterion written this way is most of the way to a test. Neither format is required. Use one when a requirement has conditions, because the structure makes you name each condition, and use a plain sentence when it doesn't.

Here is the shape of a `docs/requirements.md` for a small web project, with an ID on each requirement and a reason on each rank:

```md
# Requirements

## Problem statement
Coordinators at <partner> schedule ~40 volunteer shifts a week by email
and spreadsheet; three were double-booked last month (per the coordinator).

## Functional requirements
- FR-1 (Must: nothing else works without it): A coordinator creates a
  shift with date, time, place, headcount. Acceptance: end before start fails.
- FR-2 (Must: it prevents the double-booking): A volunteer claims an
  open shift. Acceptance: a claim on a full shift fails.
- FR-3 (Should, assumed: not yet confirmed by the coordinator): Email the
  coordinator when a claimed shift is released within 24 h of its start.

## Non-functional requirements
- NFR-1: The board loads in under 2 s on the partner's office Wi-Fi.
- NFR-2: Every action works by keyboard alone.
- NFR-3: axe-core reports no serious WCAG 2.2 AA violation.

## Constraints
- C-1: Sign-in through the partner's Google Workspace; no new accounts.
- C-2: Volunteer names and emails never leave the partner's tenant.
- C-3: Extends the partner's Django app; its CI and review rules apply.

## Success metrics
- Double-bookings in the first 4 weeks of use: 0 (from the claim log).
- Coordinator time on scheduling: under 1 h a week (the coordinator's log).

## Non-goals
- Payroll or hours export.

## Open questions
- Does v1 need recurring shifts? Owner: PM, before sprint 2.
```

Give each requirement an ID so a pull request, a test, or a commit message can cite it. Write a functional requirement as a user story where the benefit isn't obvious and as a plain statement where it is. Give every non-functional requirement a number and a way to measure it, since without both nobody can say whether it has been met.

Rank the requirements and write the rank next to each one with its reason, as the Must and Should labels above do. [MoSCoW or an impact-effort pass](/guides/planning/#prioritization-methods) is enough, because the ranking only has to tell the team what the next sprint pulls from the top.

Label a requirement nobody has confirmed as assumed, as FR-3 is, so a reader can tell a guess from a fact and knows which requirements to take back to the partner.

The non-goals section is cheap scope control, because each item costs one line and is settled before the request arrives. It turns "we didn't think of that" into "we decided against that", which moves a partner's new request from an oversight you owe them to a trade-off you can discuss.

The open questions do the same for what you don't know yet. Give each one an owner and a point in the project by which it's answered: an open question tells the reader what's still unsettled, while a confident requirement nobody checked looks settled until someone builds it.

Track the work that implements each requirement on your board rather than in a second document. [GitHub Projects](https://docs.github.com/en/issues/planning-and-tracking-with-projects/learning-about-projects/about-projects) is enough, and so is any tracker whose items can link to "FR-2". While the requirement lives in the file, the board item points at it by ID.

## Story Mapping

A user story map, a technique [Jeff Patton](https://jpattonassociates.com/story-mapping/) popularized, lays out the user's activities from left to right in the order they happen, with the stories that serve each activity stacked top to bottom by priority. A horizontal line drawn across the map marks a release: everything above it ships, and everything below it waits. The [Nielsen Norman Group's overview](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-story-mapping/) walks through a worked example.

Draw the map as a team, and with your partner if you can, because the useful part is the disagreement over where the line goes, and a map drawn by one person hides that disagreement. The stories above the first line become your functional requirements, and the line itself is the first draft of what you're committing to ship. [User Story Mapping](/activities/requirements/#user-story-mapping) is the session to run.

## Validation

A requirement is validated when someone other than its author has confirmed it's the right requirement. Two confirmations are cheap enough to run on every requirement that matters:

- Your partner reads it. Review the document with your partner, or someone else who knows the users, early and again after every reframing. A review of the written document catches a misunderstanding for the price of an hour, rather than at the demo, after the sprints built on it. Communication flaws between the project team and the customer were the second most often named critical problem in the NaPiRE survey <Cite id="mendezfernandez-2017" form="parenthetical" />.
- A prototype hits it. A clickable mockup or a thin working slice tests a requirement faster than another meeting, and it shows whether the thing is feasible at the same time.

Once a requirement is confirmed, name a test after it. A test called `test_second_claim_on_full_shift_fails` checks FR-2 on every CI run. That's verification (you built it right) rather than validation (it's the right thing to build), but it keeps a confirmed requirement from quietly breaking.

Marty Cagan argues that every product idea carries [four big risks](https://www.svpg.com/four-big-risks/): whether users will value it, whether they can use it, whether the team can build it, and whether it works for the business. Ask the same four questions of each requirement, with your partner standing in for the business. Cut or rewrite a requirement that fails one of them, because it costs as much to build as one that passes.

## Measuring Success

The problem statement says what you're fixing; success metrics say how you'll know it's fixed. Pick two or three, each with a number, a decision rule, and a collection method, and write them before you look at any data, because a metric chosen after seeing the data tends to be the one that looks good. What they count depends on the project, and the [Shipping guide](/guides/shipping/) works through four kinds: a product counts strangers who came back, a consultancy counts the partner's people using it, a FOSS contribution counts merges, and a research project answers its questions. The [Planning guide](/guides/planning/#success-metric-frameworks) has the frameworks, and [Identify Success Metrics](/activities/planning/#identify-success-metrics) is the exercise. Keep the result in `docs/requirements.md`, next to the requirements it judges.

## Best Practices for Writing Requirements

These rules keep each requirement testable and the file current:

- Back each requirement with something you can point to: a partner quote, an observed session, a competitor that does it, a number. "The coordinator asked for it" is evidence; "it seemed useful" isn't.
- Draw a wireframe or a flow diagram next to any requirement a paragraph struggles to describe, because a reader who can see the screen asks fewer questions about it.
- Update the file in the same pull request as the code that changes the behavior, so the document and the code can disagree for no longer than one review.
- Keep the problem statement and the non-goals at the top, where a reviewer, a new teammate, and your AI tools read first.
- Rank the requirements and write the reason next to the rank, so whoever reorders them later knows what they're overriding.
- Keep requirements and design decisions apart. "Must store data durably" is a requirement; "use PostgreSQL" belongs in an ADR.
- Split any requirement with "and" in it, since each half can pass or fail on its own.

## Some Truths about Software Requirements

A requirements document can be busywork, and the version that turns into busywork is easy to recognize: long, unranked, written once, and stored somewhere other than the repository. Nothing in the team's daily work touches that version, so nobody notices when it stops matching the product.

Expect much of the first version to be wrong. Requirements are guesses about what people need until a user or a partner confirms them, and the first guesses are made by a team that hasn't yet watched anyone use anything. Practitioners report the same pattern: in the NaPiRE survey, incomplete or hidden requirements was the most often named critical problem (48% of respondents), and moving targets, meaning changing goals or requirements, tied for third (33%) <Cite id="mendezfernandez-2017" form="parenthetical" />. That's why the assumed label matters: an unconfirmed requirement that looks identical to a confirmed one gets built with the same confidence.

The functional list is the part that duplicates other tools. Once your board items carry the FR IDs and their acceptance criteria, and your tests encode those criteria, the list in the file says the same thing a third time, and a copy nobody updates soon disagrees with the code. At that point, delete the functional list and replace it with a link to the board, rather than let it lag. What the board never holds is the problem statement, the non-goals, the non-functional bars, the constraints, the success metrics, and the open questions, so keep those current in the file. Letting them go stale loses the only record of what you decided not to do, what you're bound by, and how good the product has to be, and a partner is likely to ask about all three when the project ends.

One argument for heavy up-front requirements is that a problem gets more expensive to fix the longer it goes unfound. <Cite id="menzies-2017" /> tested that delayed issue effect in 171 projects and found no evidence for it: resolving an issue in a later phase didn't take consistently or substantially more effort than resolving it soon after it was introduced. Every one of those projects used the [Team Software Process](https://www.sei.cmu.edu/library/the-team-software-process-tsp/), a disciplined process built around reducing risk, and the authors don't claim the result generalizes beyond it. They suggest the effect may still hold in high-assurance software, in architecturally complex systems, and in projects with poor engineering discipline, while saying they have no evidence either way.

Write a formal, contract-like requirements document when the project is safety-critical, regulated, or built into hardware, because there a late change can mean a recall or a recertification, and catching a wrong requirement early is worth the paperwork.

## Requirements in Industry and Academia

In industry the equivalent artifact is the **product requirements document** (PRD): a product's features, behavior, constraints, and success metrics, written by a product manager for the engineers and designers who build it. Amazon starts earlier. Werner Vogels, its CTO, describes a process called [Working Backwards](https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/2006/11/working_backwards.html): the team writes the press release and the FAQ it would need at launch, then describes the customer experience and writes the user manual, and only then moves to documents closer to the implementation. Both exist for the reason your problem statement does, to force agreement on what's being built before anyone builds it.

Open source writes its requirements into proposal processes. Python's [PEP 1](https://peps.python.org/pep-0001/) asks a proposal that changes the language, its library, or its ecosystem for a motivation section explaining why what exists is inadequate for the problem it solves, and the [Rust RFC process](https://github.com/rust-lang/rfcs) asks for a written design before a substantial change is made.

In research, the equivalent is the research question and a [preregistered](https://www.cos.io/initiatives/prereg) analysis plan, written before the data is collected so the analysis can't be fitted to the results.
