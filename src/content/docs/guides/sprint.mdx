Read this before your first sprint planning and whenever a sprint ends with nothing to demo; it gives you the two-week cadence and the ceremonies that keep it on track.

A sprint is a fixed-length period during which a team plans, builds, and reviews a small, demonstrable increment of work. It is the fundamental unit of iteration in modern software engineering. Without a disciplined sprint cadence:

- Work accumulates silently until the end of the project, then collapses under its own weight.
- The team loses visibility into whether it is on track until it is too late to recover.
- The project partner is kept in the dark until a demo reveals months of misaligned assumptions.
- Individual contributions become invisible, making evaluation unreliable.

Sprints are not a bureaucratic overhead. They are a forcing function that makes progress visible, assumptions testable, and problems surfaced early.

This guide assumes **two-week sprints**, a common length for teams whose members have other commitments: long enough to finish something demonstrable, short enough that a bad plan costs two weeks rather than a month.

## Preparing for Your First Sprint

Before a team can run a real sprint (one where it plans, executes, and delivers demonstrable results) there is preparation work that must happen first. This ramp-up period has concrete deliverables:

- **Meet with your project partner** to understand the problem, constraints, and priorities.
- **Set up your development environment**: repository, CI pipeline, project board, communication channels.
- **Draft your working agreement** and Definition of Done.
- **Write initial requirements** and begin organizing a backlog.
- **Start producing tangible output early.** This is the single most important thing teams delay and later regret.

:::caution[Start Building Early]
Many teams spend the first several weeks exclusively on planning and documentation before producing any tangible output. Resist this pattern. Rough prototypes, exploratory notebooks, preliminary literature reviews, proof-of-concept spikes, and data exploration all count as building. The key is producing something concrete that the team can learn from and show to others. Start with what you know, build something small, and let early results guide your next steps.
:::

Once the team has enough context and infrastructure to pull real work items into a sprint, the preparation period is over. From that point on, every sprint should produce demonstrable progress: working software, experimental results, validated findings, or a combination.

:::note[Sprint 0]
Few teams can run a real sprint in their first week together. Between forming the team and having a repository, a backlog, and a partner who has explained the problem, the first real sprint usually starts **two to four weeks in**. That is normal, but these early weeks are not idle time. Treat them as a focused Sprint 0 with the deliverables listed above. Teams that use this time well hit the ground running; teams that treat it as free time spend the next several months catching up.
:::

## The Sprint Cycle

The vocabulary on this page comes from Scrum, and where this guide and the
[Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html) disagree, it is because
a student team is not a full-time product team: you have other courses, your
sprint length is set by an academic calendar rather than by the product, and
nobody is a dedicated Scrum Master. Read the Scrum Guide once anyway. It is short, it is the
authoritative definition of the terms your future colleagues will use, and it
is explicit that the framework is deliberately incomplete.

Each sprint follows the same four-phase cycle:

```
Plan → Build → Review → Reflect
```

### Sprint Planning

At the start of each sprint, the team meets to decide what it will accomplish by the end. A good planning session answers three questions:

1. **What is the sprint goal?** One sentence describing the most valuable thing the team will deliver this sprint.
2. **What work will we pull in?** Specific tasks selected from the backlog (user stories, research questions, experiments, data pipeline steps) sized to fit the sprint.
3. **Who owns what?** Every task has one person responsible for it by the end of the sprint.

Sprint planning should take 30-60 minutes. If it is taking longer, your backlog needs more preparation beforehand.

:::tip[The Backlog]
A backlog is the prioritized list of all work the team intends to do. Maintain it continuously: not just before planning. After every meeting with your project partner, update it. The top of the backlog should always be ready to pull into the next sprint without needing significant discussion.
:::

### The Sprint Itself

During the sprint, the team builds. Two practices keep the team coordinated without excessive meetings:

**Stand-ups**: Short syncs (10-15 minutes), two to three times per week, where each person covers:
- What I did since the last stand-up.
- What I plan to do before the next one.
- What is blocking me.

Stand-ups are not status reports for a manager. They are peer coordination. If you have nothing to report, say so; that is a signal worth surfacing. Async stand-ups (a daily thread in Discord or Teams) can supplement or replace in-person syncs when schedules conflict.

**The sprint board**: A shared visual of what is to-do, in-progress, and done. GitHub Projects, Trello, Linear, and Jira all support this. The board should reflect reality at *all times*, not just before the sprint review.

### Sprint Review

At the end of the sprint, the team demonstrates what was accomplished to the project partner and to anyone else with a stake in the direction, such as a mentor or advisor. This is the sprint demo.

The sprint review answers one question: **does the work meet the acceptance criteria and the project partner's expectations?**

Show real results, not descriptions of results. Running software on real data, notebooks with actual outputs, visualizations, benchmark comparisons, or a written literature synthesis all qualify. The common thread: demonstrate the *thing itself*, not a summary of work you did. Get explicit feedback. Adjust the backlog based on what you learn.

## Definition of Done

The Definition of Done (DoD) is the shared agreement on what it means for a piece of work to be *finished*. Without it, "done" means something different to every team member, and integration becomes painful.

A typical DoD for a **software** project:

- Code is committed to the main branch via a reviewed pull request.
- All tests pass in CI.
- The feature is deployed to a development or staging environment.
- Documentation is updated (README, inline comments, API docs if applicable).
- The project partner has been shown the feature or has signed off on the acceptance criteria.

A typical DoD for a **research** project:

- Notebooks or scripts are committed to the repository and run without errors.
- Results are reproducible: another team member can re-run the notebook and get the same output.
- Key findings, parameters, and decisions are documented (in the notebook itself or a summary document).
- Datasets are versioned or clearly referenced (source, date retrieved, preprocessing steps).
- The project partner has reviewed the results and provided feedback.

Your project may fall somewhere in between. Many research projects produce software artifacts, and many software projects include exploratory analysis. Define the DoD that fits your work. Update it as the project matures.

## Estimation and Capacity

Estimation is notoriously hard in software. The goal is not precision; it is to prevent the team from committing to more work than it can realistically complete.

A simple approach: estimate each task as **small** (a few hours), **medium** (1-2 days), or **large** (more than 2 days). Large tasks should be broken down before being pulled into a sprint.

**Capacity** is how much time the team actually has this sprint, accounting for classes, assignments in other courses, interviews, and other commitments. Count all of it during planning. Under-promising and over-delivering is better than the reverse.

After a few sprints, the team will have a sense of its **velocity**: roughly how many tasks it completes per sprint. Use this to calibrate future planning, and only for that. Velocity is a measurement of one team's throughput in that team's own units, so it does not compare across teams and it is not a productivity number. Martin Fowler's argument that [software productivity cannot be measured](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/CannotMeasureProductivity.html) is worth reading before anyone proposes tracking it.

There is a real disagreement here worth knowing about. Ron Jeffries, who helped invent story points, has since written that [he may have invented them and is sorry](https://ronjeffries.com/articles/019-01ff/story-points/Index.html), on the grounds that estimates get used as commitments and then as performance measures no matter how carefully you say they are not. The small/medium/large scheme above is deliberately coarse for that reason: it is precise enough to catch a task that will not fit in a sprint and too vague to be weaponized.

## Managing Scope Mid-Sprint

Unexpected work will arrive mid-sprint: a bug that blocks the demo, a requirement clarification from the project partner, a dependency that does not work as expected.

When this happens, the team has two options:

1. **Swap out a planned task** of similar size to accommodate the new work.
2. **Leave it for the next sprint** if it is not urgent.

Do not silently expand the sprint scope. Surfacing scope changes keeps the plan realistic and the sprint board accurate.

If a task turns out to be significantly larger than estimated, surface it at the next stand-up. Break it down, adjust the sprint, and note it in the sprint note.

## Kanban: A Complementary Approach

Not every team thrives with strict time-boxed sprints. **Kanban** is an alternative workflow model built around continuous flow rather than fixed iterations. Instead of committing to a batch of work every two weeks, the team pulls the next highest-priority item from the backlog whenever capacity opens up.

The core mechanics of Kanban are simple:

1. **Visualize the workflow.** Use a board with columns representing stages of work (e.g., Backlog → In Progress → In Review → Done).
2. **Limit work in progress (WIP).** Set a cap on how many items can be in each column at the same time. If the "In Progress" column has a WIP limit of 3, nobody starts a new task until one moves forward. This prevents the team from having ten things started and nothing finished, and it is the mechanic that makes Kanban work: [WIP limits](https://www.atlassian.com/agile/kanban/wip-limits) are what convert a board from a to-do list into a system that surfaces bottlenecks. The [Kanban Guide](https://kanbanguides.org/) is the short authoritative description.
3. **Pull, don't push.** Team members pull work when they have capacity rather than having work assigned at the start of a sprint.

### When Kanban Works Well

Kanban tends to suit teams that:

- Are **self-driven and proactive**. In a sprint, the planning meeting assigns work explicitly. In Kanban, every team member is responsible for pulling the next task without being asked. This only works when every person on the team takes ownership of finding and starting work independently.
- Have unpredictable workloads (e.g., frequent project partner feedback that reshuffles priorities).
- Prefer a steady flow of small deliverables over batch delivery every two weeks.
- Find sprint boundaries artificial or stressful given the pace of their other commitments.

:::caution[Kanban Requires More Initiative, Not Less]
A common failure mode: a team adopts Kanban and one or more members stop contributing because "there was nothing to do" or "I was waiting to be assigned something." In a pull-based system, the backlog *is* the assignment. If the board has items in the backlog column and you are idle, you pull the next one. No one is going to tap you on the shoulder. Teams where members are not yet comfortable taking initiative on their own are usually better served by sprint planning, where work is explicitly discussed and distributed.
:::

### Scrumban: The Hybrid

Many student teams land somewhere in between, and that is fine. **Scrumban** combines the sprint cadence (for planning and demos) with Kanban-style flow (WIP limits, continuous pulling) during the sprint itself. In practice this looks like:

- The team still plans every two weeks and runs a sprint review and retrospective.
- During the sprint, work flows continuously rather than being frozen into a fixed commitment.
- WIP limits prevent overload and keep the team focused on finishing over starting.

This hybrid is often the most practical fit for student teams: the sprint boundaries give a regular rhythm for written progress notes and project partner check-ins, while the Kanban flow gives flexibility for the uneven schedules of student life.

:::tip[Pick What Works, Then Adapt]
You do not need to choose a methodology on day one and stick with it forever. Start with basic sprints. If the team struggles with rigid commitments, experiment with WIP limits and continuous flow. The retrospective is where you decide what to change. The only wrong approach is one you never revisit.
:::

## What Good Sprints Look Like

At the end of a good sprint:

- The sprint goal was met or the team can clearly explain why it was not.
- Real results were demonstrated to the project partner: running software, notebook outputs, validated findings.
- At least one process improvement was identified and committed to.
- Every team member made a visible, documented contribution.

At the end of a struggling sprint:

- Most tasks are "still in progress."
- The demo was skipped or replaced with a summary of what was *attempted* rather than what was *achieved*.
- The same blockers appear sprint after sprint.
- The retrospective produces the same action items with no follow-through.

The difference is usually not raw effort: it is planning fidelity, stand-ups that surface blockers, and retrospective follow-through.

## Tools

Every tool below is replaceable and none of them will save a team that does not maintain its board. Pick the smallest set that covers planning, a visible board, and a record of what happened, and treat anything beyond that as optional.

| Need | Options |
|------|---------|
| Sprint board / backlog | GitHub Projects (recommended), Linear, Jira, Trello, Notion |
| Stand-up async | MS Teams or Discord (daily thread) |
| Time tracking (optional) | Toggl, Clockify |
| Notebooks / experiments | Jupyter, Google Colab, Observable |
| CI for sprint proof | GitHub Actions, CircleCI, GitLab CI |

Choose tools your team will actually use. A simple GitHub Projects board maintained diligently beats a sophisticated Jira setup that nobody updates, and the maintenance discipline, not the feature set, is what decides whether the board tells you the truth at planning time.

:::note[Reviewed annually]
The tool names above date faster than anything else on this page. Checked at the annual review.
:::

## Best Practices

- Commit to a sprint goal, not just a task list. Goals are more resilient to mid-sprint disruptions.
- Do not start new tasks if blocked ones are still open. Finish what is in progress before starting something new.
- Demo to your project partner every sprint, not just at major milestones. Frequent feedback is cheaper than late surprises.
- Keep sprint planning lightweight. An over-engineered planning process reduces the time available to actually build things.
- Timebox stand-ups. If they are running long, take detailed discussions offline.

## Some Truths About Sprints

Start with the uncomfortable one: a two-week sprint on a student team is a
simulation of a practice designed for people who work on one thing full time.
Your sprint contains four other courses, two midterms, and a week where nobody
is available. Real teams have variable capacity too, but not by a factor of
three. This matters because the ceremony most likely to feel like theater,
sprint planning, is the one that becomes useful only when capacity is
stable enough to learn from. Expect the first two or three sprints to be
calibration rather than prediction, and do not conclude that the practice is
worthless when sprint one overruns.

Velocity is where this goes wrong most often. It is a planning aid measured in
one team's own arbitrary units, and the moment anyone compares it between teams
or uses it to assess an individual, the people producing the estimates start
protecting themselves and the numbers stop meaning anything. This is not a
hypothetical failure: it is common enough in industry that Jeffries, who helped
popularize the unit, now argues against it. If you track velocity at all, track
it to answer "did we take on too much" and nothing else.

Kanban is frequently adopted for the wrong reason. It is a genuine alternative
with its own literature and its own discipline, but on a student team the
proposal to switch usually arrives right after a sprint the team failed to
finish, and what is actually being proposed is the removal of the deadline that
exposed the problem. Kanban without WIP limits and without continuous
prioritization is not Kanban, it is an untracked to-do list. Switch because
your work arrives unpredictably, which is true for some consultancy
and FOSS teams, not because committing to a sprint goal turned out to be
uncomfortable.

The ceremony teams drop first is the retrospective, and it is the one with the
best return. Planning and review produce visible artifacts, so skipping them
feels like skipping work; the retro produces a conversation, so skipping it
feels like saving an hour. What you actually lose is the only scheduled moment
where a process problem can be named by the person experiencing it. A team that
has quietly stopped running retros usually has an unspoken problem, and the retro
is how it would have surfaced.

Finally, the whole framework is a means. The [Agile Manifesto](https://agilemanifesto.org/)
is twelve principles and four value statements, and it is worth reading precisely
because it is so much smaller than the industry built on top of it. Its
[principles](https://agilemanifesto.org/principles.html) say nothing about story
points, burndown charts, or two-week iterations. Adapt the ceremonies to your
team; keep the goal, which is a working increment and a frank conversation
about it, every two weeks.

## Sprints in Industry and Academia

Sprints originated in Scrum, formalized in the early 1990s by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland. Kanban was adapted for software development by David Anderson in the mid-2000s, drawing on Toyota's manufacturing flow system. Most software companies today use some variation of one or both, even if they do not use those names.

In practice, industry teams customize heavily: some use one-week sprints, others four-week cycles; some run pure Kanban with no iteration boundaries; many land on a hybrid. The underlying principle (short iterations, frequent feedback, continuous improvement) is nearly universal.

In academic capstone projects, the sprint cadence serves an additional purpose: it creates a documented record of contributions over time, which is the fairest basis anyone has for judging who did what. Even teams that prefer Kanban-style flow should keep regular demos and retrospectives to satisfy this need.

:::note[Process Overhead]
Sprint ceremonies take time, and it is worth knowing roughly how much. For a two-week sprint, the approximate per-person overhead averages out to:

| Activity | Per sprint | Weekly average |
|----------|-----------|----------------|
| Sprint planning | 30-60 min | ~15-30 min |
| Stand-ups (2-3x/week) | 10-15 min each | ~30-45 min |
| Sprint review / demo | 30-60 min | ~15-30 min |
| Retrospective | 30-45 min | ~15-22 min |
| Board and backlog upkeep | ongoing | ~20-30 min |

That totals roughly **1.5 to 2.5 hours per week** on process, leaving the majority of your project hours for building. For comparison, the [Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html) caps a two-week sprint at about ten hours of ceremonies per person (four hours planning, two hours review, ninety minutes retrospective, fifteen minutes a day), roughly 12% of a full-time fortnight, and most teams run well under the ceiling.

These are averages. Some weeks will be heavier (planning week, demo prep) and others lighter. The workload also shifts across a long project: the early months carry more ramp-up overhead, the middle is the most build-intensive, and the final stretch focuses on refinement with naturally lighter sprints. If your process overhead starts crowding out build time, simplify your ceremonies rather than skipping them.
:::

## Additional Readings

Scrum, Kanban, and the agile literature around them are unusually
well-documented at the primary-source level, and the primary sources are short.
Read the Scrum Guide and the Kanban Guide before reading anything written about
them; almost every disagreement you will encounter is about material those two
documents do not actually contain.

**Sources and further reading**

- [The Scrum Guide](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html), Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland: the authoritative definition, about thirteen pages.
- [The Kanban Guide](https://kanbanguides.org/): the equivalent short definition for flow-based work.
- [Manifesto for Agile Software Development](https://agilemanifesto.org/) and its [twelve principles](https://agilemanifesto.org/principles.html): the original document, much smaller than the industry built on it.
- [Story Points Revisited](https://ronjeffries.com/articles/019-01ff/story-points/Index.html), Ron Jeffries: one of the inventors arguing against the practice, and why.
