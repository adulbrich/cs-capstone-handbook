You are enrolled in CS 461, 462, and 463, the on-campus, three-term computer science capstone. One team, one project partner or mentor, one project for the whole year.

Other capstone options (online CS 46X, the one-term CS 467, ECE 441/442/443, ENGR 415/416) are separate sections. The on-campus lectures share a time slot, so switching between CS, ECE, and ENGR is easy.

## How You Get Your Project and Team

Project partners (companies, nonprofits, faculty, staff, entrepreneurs) and students submit projects through the same portal. The instruction team reviews each one and lists the approved projects before fall week 0. You then rank them in a bidding survey before the term starts, and teams are announced in fall week 1.

- **Bidding is the default.** You rank the projects and we do our best to place you on a top choice. That is not a guarantee.
- **Teams are 3 to 4 students** in the usual case. Real teams run from 2 to 5 depending on enrollment and what the project needs, and the instruction team balances skills and preferences across all of them.
- **Friends.** Rank the same projects in the same order. Still not a guarantee. The only reliable way to be on a team with your friends is to propose a project together before the term starts.
- **You can propose your own project**, of any kind, but it needs a project partner or an approved mentor attached, and that mentor (or an instructor, where there is no outside mentor) acts as your project partner for the year, evaluations included. If it is accepted, you and any teammates named in the proposal are assigned to it automatically, and it may never appear in the public list. Submissions close when fall term starts, and every proposal declares whether the project requires [an IP agreement, an NDA, or both](/introduction/ip-nda-and-licensing/). If your project has not a full team yet, we still ask you to complete the regular bidding survey. If your project is not picked by enough students, you'll be placed on another project.
- **Reserving a spot** is not possible, with two exceptions: you submitted the project, or your project partner submitted it in collaboration with you.
- **Asking a partner beforehand.** Some project descriptions invite you to contact the partner with questions. That is information, not a reservation; the instruction team places students.
- **Every team has an external partner.** Some projects arrive with one. For the rest, the team finds one or more mentors (faculty, industry professionals, or anyone who can move the project forward), approved by the instruction team, and a mentor's evaluation carries the same weight as a project partner's. Align expectations with them in your first meeting.
- **Several teams on one project.** Some partners take more than one team. What your team owns, which interface, repository, or milestone, and how the teams talk to each other, is negotiated with the partner rather than assumed, and each team writes its own part into its [Definition of Shipped](/assignments/definition-of-shipped/).
- **Outside the class.** Your team may collaborate with a separate ECE capstone team on a shared project, with a research group, or with people from other colleges, professional organizations, and online communities. Joining an ECE or multidisciplinary (ENGR) team outright means enrolling in that section instead of this one.

## What You Do Each Term

- **Fall:** bid on a project, [meet your partner](/assignments/term-startup/), write the team charter, and build a walking skeleton. You defend your own contributions live, and you write an RFC.
- **Winter:** build and verify against real users; define what "shipped" means for your project and run an incident postmortem. Another RFC, another defense.
- **Spring:** release, measure, publish a landing page, hand the project off, and present at the Engineering Expo. A career retrospective closes the year.

Every term, your grade is four equal components, none of which can be crammed in week 10. The [assignments overview](/assignments/introduction/) has the components, weights, and due weeks.

## Lectures

Each week's slot is a workshop, a guest speaker (industry, faculty, alumni who shipped something), a demo day, or no lecture at all. The [schedule](/introduction/schedule/) says which, what is due, and what to read first. Check Canvas for announcements, meeting times, and room.

## If the Project or the Team Has to Change

Both are possible, and both need the instruction team's approval, because both disrupt more than your own team.

**Changing teams** happens only when the alternative is worse. If a concern is building, talk to your Teaching Assistant (TA) or an instructor as soon as possible: most team problems are solved earlier and more cheaply through the [conflict guide](/guides/conflict/) than through a transfer. Reasons that have justified a change are a conflict mediation could not resolve, a skills mismatch large enough that you would clearly contribute more elsewhere, and a multi-team project that needs its people rebalanced. Write to the instructor with the reasons and what you already tried inside the team. The instructor talks to everyone involved and decides. If it is approved, both teams agree a transition plan covering responsibilities, knowledge, and in-flight work, and the reasons, the decision, and the plan are written down. You are then assessed on your contributions to each team for the time you were on it, and the grade may be adjusted for the impact of the change on both projects.

**Pivoting or changing the project** may also mean finding a new project partner or mentor. Teams pivot for reasons like ill-defined requirements, no product-market fit, a problem that turns out to be infeasible, data that cannot be obtained, a disagreement over IP terms, or an irreparable conflict with the partner. Your grade is based on the project you present at each milestone, against the same standards throughout, so the later you pivot the less runway you have and teams that wait until winter or spring rarely recover. If you suspect a dead end, say so in your next sprint note and TA check-in. To pivot: write down the current path, why it is not viable, and how the new direction fixes that; revise the scope, timeline, and roles for the time that is left; get the instruction team's approval and bring your project partner or mentor along, because they evaluate your work; then restart from whichever point the new direction requires, and expect the extra effort.

**Abandoning a project** is rare and usually means the project should not have been approved. A team that invested substantial effort before external factors beyond its control ended the project may receive partial credit, on the strength of an exhaustive post-mortem submitted to the instruction team.

## Questions Students Ask

### Could this become a company?

Then build it in the open and ship it. You [hold the IP](/introduction/ip-nda-and-licensing/) by default, you may keep the code closed, and the [Project Handoff](/assignments/project-handoff/) lets the successor be you. What is rewarded is a deployed product with users you do not know, not a secret; the [Shipping guide](/guides/shipping/#new-product-or-game) has that path.

### Can I reuse my honors thesis?

Probably not. Ask the instruction team if you think there is a match.

## Resources

Project funds and cloud access go through the instruction team; the request form is on Canvas.

- Hardware, software licenses, user research, and light marketing: a small budget plus a hardware inventory, all subject to approval.
- AWS: shared accounts with a budget cap, wiped at the end of the year, on request.
- Azure: some OSU departments host projects built for them; the department, not the instruction team, decides.
- [HPC](https://it.engineering.oregonstate.edu/hpc) and [iLabs](https://engineering.oregonstate.edu/tools-services/iLabs): open to every engineering student after the mandatory training.
