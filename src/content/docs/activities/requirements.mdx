Your requirements are one of the key pieces of documentation that will create a shared understanding of what needs to be built, and the problem being solved. These activities will help you frame your requirements.

They are also load-bearing for the graded work: your requirements live in the repository as continuously updated docs, and the user-facing ones determine whether you can meet the winter real-user gate. Before running any interview-based activity on this page, read the [Mom Test cheat sheet](/the-mom-test-cheat-sheet.md); it is the difference between compliments and evidence. Then check the [Shipping guide](/guides/shipping/) and the [Definition of Shipped](/assignments/definition-of-shipped/) assignment to see where your requirements work is heading.

> "The hardest single part of building a software system is deciding precisely what to build." -- Fred Brooks

Attend the OSU Advantage Accelerator's [Iterate](https://advantage.oregonstate.edu/advantage-accelerator/programs/iterate) workshop to pressure-test your project idea as a venture. Sessions run periodically over Zoom; check the page for the next date and register.

Check some of the Accelerator's [other resources](https://advantage.oregonstate.edu/innovation-impact-program/student-resources).

A good output is the list of hypotheses the session changed, each with the user interviews you scheduled to test it.

## Hypothesis Testing

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/> <Badge text="Team Activity" variant="note" class="mb-6" /> <Badge text="Recommended" variant="tip" class="mb-6"/>

Most of the information in your **Lean Canvas** is hypotheses. Identify five of the riskiest hypotheses in your plan's customer segments, problems, and competition. 

For each hypothesis:

1. Construct a fail/pass test.
2. Interview at least five members of the relevant customer segment (enough to suggest the hypothesis holds or not) and produce anonymized data about the customer interviewed. Run the interviews with the [Mom Test cheat sheet](/the-mom-test-cheat-sheet.md) in hand: ask about past behavior, not hypothetical enthusiasm.
3. Write a clear and professional summary of the results of the test (insights, etc.)
4. If your initial hypothesis is false, propose a pivot hypothesis.

If working on this activity as a team, have each person focus on a different set of hypotheses. The interviewees you recruit here are candidates for your [Definition of Shipped](/assignments/definition-of-shipped/) user access plan and the winter real-user gate.

A good output is five riskiest hypotheses, each with its pass/fail test, anonymized data from at least five interviews, and a written result including a pivot wherever the hypothesis failed.

**Feeds:** the [Definition of Shipped](/assignments/definition-of-shipped/) target rung, and the [repo checkpoint](/assignments/repo-checkpoints/) living docs.

## User Story Mapping

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/> <Badge text="Team Activity" variant="note" class="mb-6" /> <Badge text="Workshop" variant="caution" class="mb-6"/>

After reading this [User Story Mapping](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/user-story-mapping/) article from the Nielsen Norman Group, create a comprehensive user story map for your project, and include an initial written set of epics and user stories. This will help define your use cases and scenarios.

If working on this activity as a team, have each person focus on a different set of stories. Indicate whether each user story is high or low level. 

### INVEST Criteria

When writing user stories, ensure they meet the INVEST criteria:

- **Independent**: Stories should be self-contained, allowing them to be developed and delivered independently.
- **Negotiable**: Stories are not contracts; they should be flexible and open to discussion.
- **Valuable**: Each story should deliver value to the end-user or customer.
- **Estimable**: Stories should be clear enough to estimate the effort/time required for implementation.
- **Small**: Stories should be small enough to be completed within a single iteration or sprint.
- **Testable**: There should be clear acceptance criteria to determine when a story is complete and meets expectations.

In the workshop's second half, produce the same story map with an AI agent (a skill or a structured prompt) and set it beside yours; the differences are the lesson, and either version can be the one you keep.

A good output is a user story map with epics and written user stories, each marked as in or out of scope for this year.

**Feeds:** `docs/requirements.md` for every [repo checkpoint](/assignments/repo-checkpoints/), and the winter [Definition of Shipped](/assignments/definition-of-shipped/) target rung.

## User's Emotional Objectives

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/>

We often focus on users' functional goals, but emotional objectives often play a bigger role in decision-making.

- What emotions is the user seeking when using the software?
- How does the software impact their relationships or self-perception?

For example, a peer resume critique website might meet the functional goal of reviewing resumes, but the emotional objective is to ease job search anxiety. Similarly, in the auto industry, messaging focuses on emotions: a car might make someone feel wealthy or adventurous. How can your software address these emotional needs?

Write an analysis of your project from the perspective of the user emotional objectives, explaining which user segment or audience you are targeting. Share it with your team.

A good output is a written list of the emotional objectives behind your users' functional goals, and one design decision each one changes.

## Domain Model

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/>

Create a [Domain Model](https://simplea.com/Articles/domain-model) that represents the key entities and relationships within your project's problem space.

- Identify Entities: List the main objects or concepts relevant to the domain (e.g., users, products, orders).
- Define Attributes: For each entity, specify key characteristics or properties (e.g., name, ID, status).
- Establish Relationships: Map how entities interact with or relate to one another (e.g., “User places Order”).
- Draw the Model: Use diagrams (UML or similar) to visualize the domain.

A good output is a diagram along with a brief explanation of the entities and their relationships.

## User Manual

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/> <Badge text="Team Activity" variant="note" class="mb-6" /> <Badge text="Recommended" variant="tip" class="mb-6"/>

Develop a preliminary user manual that outlines how end-users will interact with your software.

- Identify Key Features: List the core features users need to know about.
- User Instructions: Provide step-by-step guides on how to use the system, including screen navigation and input details.
- Common Issues and Solutions: Include troubleshooting tips for common problems users may face.
- Interface Overview: Provide a brief description of the user interface.

If working on this activity as a team, have each person focus on a different subset of the manual.

A good output is a draft of the user manual with clear instructions on using the main features of your system.

**Feeds:** the [Project Handoff](/assignments/project-handoff/), where real users remain.

## Define Your Research Questions

<Badge text="Team Activity" variant="note" class="mb-6"/> <Badge text="Recommended" variant="tip" class="mb-6"/>

For research and R&D projects, this does the job user stories do for everyone else: it decides what you build and how you will know it worked. One to two hours with your faculty partner, after a [literature review](/activities/creative/#write-a-literature-review).

- **Write the question** in one sentence, specific enough to be answerable in three terms. "Can transformer models help with X" is not a question; "does fine-tuning on Y reduce false negatives on Z below the current baseline" is. This [guide to writing research questions](https://research.com/research/how-to-write-a-research-question) helps if you are starting cold.
- **Say whether the research is primary or secondary**: are you collecting original data, or analyzing data someone else collected? The answer changes your whole timeline, because primary research usually means [an IRB application](/activities/user/#complete-an-irb-application).
- **State the hypothesis and its negation**: what result would support it, and what result would refute it. If no observable result would change your mind, you have a topic rather than a hypothesis.
- **Name the baseline** you will compare against, with a citation and a code link if one exists.
- **Name the metric** and the threshold that counts as success. Agree it with your partner now, in writing.
- **Write down what you are not asking.** Research scope creep is worse than product scope creep, because it stays invisible until the term runs out.

A good output is a page in `docs/requirements.md` carrying the question, whether it is primary or secondary, the hypothesis with its refutation condition, the named baseline with a citation, and the success metric your faculty partner agreed to.

**Feeds:** the [repo checkpoint](/assignments/repo-checkpoints/) living-docs criterion, and the winter [Definition of Shipped](/assignments/definition-of-shipped/) target rung.

## Collect Data Sources

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/>

If you are conducting secondary research, i.e., analyzing existing data or literature collected by others, list all the relevant data sources needed to answer your research questions.

Specifically, list the data sources, who created them, when, their format/schema, how to access or download them, and what their purpose is in the context of your research.

A good output is a table of every data source with its creator, date, format, access method, and role in answering your research question.

## Define The Data To Collect

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/>

If you are [conducting primary research](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/research_and_citation/conducting_research/conducting_primary_research/index.html), i.e., collecting original data, define what type of data (i.e., qualitative, quantitative, mixed) you need to collect, and what methodologies you will use (i.e., surveys/questionnaires, interviews or focus groups, experiments or observations). Explain the purpose of each category of data collected with regard to your research question(s).

A good output is a written data collection plan naming the data types, the methodologies, and the purpose of each category against your research questions.

## Low or Medium-Fidelity Prototype

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/>

Create a low or medium-fidelity prototype to clarify and validate the project's functional requirements.

- Define Key Features: Identify the main features to be included in the prototype.
- Create the Prototype: Use tools like Figma, [Penpot](https://penpot.app/), or similar to build a clickable prototype that demonstrates key user flows.
- Validate Requirements: Review the prototype with stakeholders or users to ensure it meets functional requirements.

Read this [blog post about a SoundCloud iOS redesign](https://www.freecodecamp.org/news/my-friends-hate-soundcloud-ios-so-i-redesigned-it-for-them-d3038cdd020b).

A good output is the prototype link and a brief reflection on how it clarified or influenced the project's requirements.

## Jobs to Be Done Theory

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/>

The [Jobs to Be Done (JTBD) Theory](https://www.coursera.org/articles/jobs-to-be-done) is a framework for understanding the underlying tasks or "jobs" users are trying to accomplish with your product. Instead of focusing solely on features, JTBD helps you uncover the real motivations and desired outcomes of your users.

1. Identify a primary user segment for your project.
2. Interview or observe users to understand what "job" they are hiring your product to do.
3. Write at least two JTBD statements in the format:  
   *"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome] without [pain point]."*

Capture your JTBD statements and a brief explanation of how these insights will influence your requirements or design decisions. Interview with the [Mom Test cheat sheet](/the-mom-test-cheat-sheet.md) in hand.

A good output is the job statement for your primary user segment, backed by what you heard in interviews or observation, and the features it reprioritizes.

## Current vs. Ideal Workflow

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/>

Analyzing the current workflow versus the ideal workflow helps you identify pain points, inefficiencies, and opportunities for improvement in your project's domain.

1. Document the current workflow for your target users as a step-by-step process.
2. Identify bottlenecks, frustrations, or areas for improvement.
3. Describe or diagram the ideal workflow that your solution aims to enable.

A good output is a comparison of the current and ideal workflows (as text or diagrams), highlighting key differences and how your project addresses the gaps.

## Is / Is-Not

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/>

The Is / Is-Not analysis helps clarify the scope and boundaries of your project by explicitly stating what the project is and is not.

1. Create a table or list with two columns: "Is" and "Is-Not".
2. In the "Is" column, list characteristics, features, or goals that are in scope for your project.
3. In the "Is-Not" column, list items that are out of scope or explicitly excluded.

A good output is your Is / Is-Not table or list, with a short explanation of how this analysis will help keep your project focused and aligned. An explicit "Is-Not" column makes it much easier to write an honest target rung in your [Definition of Shipped](/assignments/definition-of-shipped/).
