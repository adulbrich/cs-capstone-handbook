Reflective activities help you apply what you learn to your project. Regular reflection also gives you concrete material for any retrospective you have to write, about the project or about your own growth in it.

> "The unexamined life is not worth living" (Socrates)

## Analyze an Existing Codebase

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/> <Badge text="Team Activity" variant="note" class="mb-6"/> <Badge text="Recommended" variant="tip" class="mb-6"/>

Learn how the code you're inheriting or contributing to is actually built, tested, and reviewed before you change any of it: a predecessor team's repository, your partner's codebase, or the upstream FOSS project. Two to three hours, once, in your first weeks on the codebase: alone to learn a project, as a team when the audit is due. If your project starts from scratch, run it on a FOSS project close to yours instead; the practices you find are the ones to adopt.

- **Run it**: fresh clone, follow their README or handoff document to a working build and a passing test run, and write down every step that failed or was undocumented.
- **Read the process**: CONTRIBUTING file, review rules, CI configuration, branch and release conventions, issue templates; for a predecessor team, their handoff document, project retrospective, and risk register.
- **Map the state**: which tests exist and pass, how old the dependencies are, what the last ten merged changes touched and who reviewed them, where the known debt sits.
- **Decide first moves**: what you adopt as is, what you change first and why, and what you won't touch this year.

A good output is a one-page audit of the codebase you're inheriting: how it builds, how it's tested, how a change gets reviewed and released, what is undocumented, and what surprised you, ending with the first three changes you intend to make.

## Read ACM Queue Articles

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/>

Find an [ACM Queue Article](https://queue.acm.org/) of some relevance to your project, read it, and write a summary of what you learned that could or should be applied to your project. Include specifics. Share it with your team.

A good output is a written summary, shared with your team, naming specifics from the article and what they change about your project.

## Watch a Documentary

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/> <Badge text="Team Activity" variant="note" class="mb-6" />

Watch a documentary film related to your project. Each team member writes a paragraph about a different aspect of the film that is relevant to the project.

Here are some ideas:

- [The Social Dilemma](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt11464826/)
- [Coded Bias](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt11394170/)
- [The Thinking Game](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt32150119/) (on DeepMind, AlphaFold, and the race toward general AI)
- [Eternal You](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt30320493/) (on AI avatars of the dead and the grief-tech industry)
- [Design Disruptors](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt8189076/)
- [The Great Hack](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt4736550/)
- [AlphaGo](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WXuK6gekU1Y)
- [Inside Envoy: The Proxy for the Future](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uaksVVHDhYU)
- [Prometheus: The Documentary](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rT4fJNbfe14)
- [How A Small Team of Developers Created React at Facebook | React.js: The Documentary](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=8pDqJVdNa44)

Documentaries lag the technology by a few years, so if you want something closer to the current moment, a recent long-form piece (video essay, in-depth podcast, or feature article) on the social impact of large language models and generative AI also works; agree on it as a team first.

A good output is one paragraph per team member, each on a different aspect of the film, saying what it means for your project.

## External Feedback Session

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/> <Badge text="Recommended" variant="tip" class="mb-6"/>

Find someone who has relevant expertise to review your project. It can be a technical person or a knowledge domain expert. The person should have little knowledge of your project. 

Present the relevant parts of your project to them and collect their feedback. Write a summary of what you learned that could or should be applied to your project. Include specifics. Share it with your team.

A good output is a written summary of the feedback with the specific changes you'll make, shared with your team.

## Learning Journal

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/> <Badge text="Recommended" variant="tip" class="mb-6"/>

A learning journal is a personal record where you reflect on your learning experiences, capturing insights, challenges, and progress over time. It's all about metacognition (thinking about thinking).

1. Write weekly reflections on what you learned, challenges faced, and how you overcame them.
2. Relate new knowledge to past experiences.
3. Evaluate your skills and identify areas for improvement.

A term of journal entries is material for any retrospective you have to write: instead of reconstructing ten weeks from memory, you quote yourself.

A good output is a term of weekly entries you can quote from directly.

## Keep an Experiment Log

<Badge text="Individual Activity" variant="success" class="mb-6"/> <Badge text="Recommended" variant="tip" class="mb-6"/>

Record every run you make, so that at the end you can say what you tried and what it showed instead of reconstructing six months from shell history. Ten minutes per experiment.

- **One entry per run**: date, the question it was meant to answer, the exact configuration and commit hash, the result, and what you concluded.
- **Log the failures too.** The runs that went nowhere are the ones you'll otherwise repeat, and they're what a paper's limitations section is made of.
- **Note what you changed** relative to the previous run, one thing at a time wherever you can.
- **Review the log at every sprint boundary** and pull the interesting entries into your sprint note.

A good output is a running log in which any entry can be traced to a commit and re-run.
