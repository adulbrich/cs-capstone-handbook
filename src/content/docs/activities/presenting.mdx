Presenting activities prepare you for any session where you explain your work out loud and answer for it: a design review, a demo, a conference talk. The last one puts your work in front of someone with relevant expertise and little knowledge of your project, who will see what the team has stopped noticing.

> "The great enemy of communication, we find, is the illusion of it." (William H. Whyte, "Is Anybody Listening?", *Fortune*, 1950)


## Learn from Jean-luc Doumont

<ActivityMeta anchor="learn-from-jean-luc-doumont" effort="1 to 2 h" />

Two or three techniques for structuring a talk or a document, applied to one of your own. Jean-luc Doumont teaches STEM professionals how to organize and present ideas; his book *Trees, Maps, and Theorems* and his free [handouts and videos](https://www.principiae.be/) (also on [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/@jeanlucdoumont)) are the source. One to two hours, once.

Read his book or find handouts or videos relevant to your situation or concerns, then write a brief summary of what you learned and what you'll apply to your presentation or document. Sharing your revised presentation or document is a plus.

A good output is a short write-up of the two or three techniques you'll actually use, with one slide or paragraph you rewrote using them.

## Learn from Patrick Winston

<ActivityMeta anchor="learn-from-patrick-winston" />

One concrete change to your next presentation, taken from the late AI researcher Patrick Winston's lecture [How to Speak](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Unzc731iCUY). Watch it (about an hour), then write a brief summary of what you learned and what you'll apply.

> "Your success in life will be determined largely by your ability to speak, your ability to write, and the quality of your ideas. In that order." (Patrick Henry Winston)

A good output is a brief summary naming what you learned and the one thing you'll change in your next presentation.

## Learn from TED Presentation Advice

<ActivityMeta anchor="learn-from-ted-presentation-advice" />

Here are three resources about doing a [TED Talk](https://www.ted.com/):

- [How to Give a Killer Presentation](https://hbr.org/2013/06/how-to-give-a-killer-presentation) (article)
- [The Secret Structure of Great Talks](https://www.ted.com/talks/nancy_duarte_the_secret_structure_of_great_talks?) (video)
- [Doing a TED Talk: The Full Story](https://waitbutwhy.com/2016/03/doing-a-ted-talk-the-full-story.html) (article)

Learn from them or find other resources (videos, etc.), then write a brief summary of what you learned and what you'll apply to your presentation.

A good output is a short write-up of what you took from the resources and how it changes the structure of your next talk.

## Conquer your Fear of Public Speaking

<ActivityMeta anchor="conquer-your-fear-of-public-speaking" />

Research tips to help you become more comfortable and confident in front of an audience. Practice a few of these techniques, then write a brief summary of what you learned and how you plan to incorporate them into your presentation.

Here are a couple of links to get you started:

- [Before Public Speaking...](https://www.ted.com/playlists/226/before_public_speaking) (video)
- [5 Ways to Get Over Your Fear of Public Speaking](https://hbr.org/2018/02/5-ways-to-get-over-your-fear-of-public-speaking) (article)

A good output is a brief summary of the techniques you practiced and which ones you'll use before your next presentation.

## External Feedback Session

<ActivityMeta anchor="external-feedback-session" />

Find someone who has relevant expertise to review your project. It can be a technical person or a knowledge domain expert. The person should have little knowledge of your project. 

Present the relevant parts of your project to them and collect their feedback. Write a summary of what you learned that could or should be applied to your project. Include specifics. Share it with your team.

A good output is a written summary of the feedback with the specific changes you'll make, shared with your team.
