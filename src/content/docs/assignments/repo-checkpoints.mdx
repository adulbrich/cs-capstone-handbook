<AssignmentSummary />

Your requirements and design documents live in your repository under `docs/`, stay current because your team (and your AI tools) need them current, and are graded at the commit you name: the documents from the PDF you submit, and the build, the term gate, and the history from the repository at that commit. A checkpoint is the instruction team's review of the repository as it actually is, with no drift between the document and the code. Graders may leave issues or PR comments in your repository as feedback.

## What You Produce

The repository holds what an AI tool or a successor needs to work on the product, and nothing about the team itself, because your partner reads it and whoever continues the project inherits it. Your team's documents (the charter, the retrospectives, the Definition of Shipped, and a postmortem on anything but a technical incident) live where your team keeps its own files, such as a shared drive or wiki, and each one is submitted as a PDF. The [handoff document](/assignments/project-handoff/) goes where your partner keeps project documents.

- `docs/requirements.md`: what the product must do and how you will know, with acceptance criteria for current priorities, the limits you did not choose (an existing database, data rules, a license, an inherited codebase's CI, review rules, and hosting) under Constraints, the non-goals, the success metrics, and the open product questions. Updated as scope changes; git history is the change log. The [requirements guide](/guides/requirements/) has the shape of a requirement a teammate can test.
- `docs/design.md`: the system view someone would need to contribute safely: context, key diagrams, interfaces and contracts, the data model, deployment shape, the design decisions deferred on purpose, and the open technical questions to spike. Product scope stays in the requirements and the reasons for a decision in its record. The [technical design guide](/guides/technical-design/) says what the document must decide and how to keep it current.
- `docs/rfcs/` or `docs/adr/`: recorded decisions, each with what was rejected and why; the design document links them where they apply rather than listing them. Either directory is fine, and so is both; if your tooling already writes to `docs/adr/` (several published agent skill sets do by default), use it. See the [ADR guide](/guides/adr/).
- `docs/risks.md`: one risk register for the whole project (business, technical, and team risks together), ranked, with owners, and listing every external prerequisite you need (an approval, an account, a review, recruitment) with who asked for it and when. This is the only risk register; do not maintain copies elsewhere. Your partner and the next team read it, so write each risk as a fact about the project, without blame ("partner review takes two weeks; we plan around it"). A problem between teammates goes to your Teaching Assistant (TA), not into the register. The [Project Risks guide](/guides/risks/) shows how to write and rank each entry.
- `README.md`: a quickstart that a new contributor (or grader) can actually follow. [makeareadme.com](https://www.makeareadme.com/) lists what a reader looks for.
- [`CONTRIBUTING.md`](https://docs.github.com/en/communities/setting-up-your-project-for-healthy-contributions/setting-guidelines-for-repository-contributors), carrying your Definition of Done and Contribution Norm, and the AI context file (`AGENTS.md`, `CLAUDE.md`, or your tool's equivalent), which points at the documents above rather than copying them, both from your [Team Charter](/assignments/team-charter/). [`AGENTS.md`](https://agents.md/) is the cross-tool convention and the safest default; per-tool files should point at it rather than duplicate it, because two instruction files disagreeing with each other is worse than none.

**AI use:** AI may draft and maintain the living docs from your code and history, and may write the CI configuration; the checkpoint grades the repository, not who typed it. What fails the checkpoint is evidence that is not real: a green badge from a workflow that runs nothing, a requirements document describing features the code does not have, or a PR history dressed up after the fact.

## What CI Must Do

Every project needs [continuous integration](https://martinfowler.com/articles/continuousIntegration.html), in whatever form fits where your code lives. The minimum is four outcomes:

1. Every change reaches the default branch through a pull request that someone other than the author approved.
2. An automated check runs on every pull request, and it can actually fail. Break something on a branch once and watch it go red, so you know.
3. The check runs your tests, and at least one test fails if the behavior it covers breaks.
4. A stranger can go from a clean clone to a running system using only the README.

What that looks like depends on the project:

| If your project is | Realization |
|---|---|
| New Product or Game, Consultancy | A workflow that runs on every pull request: it builds with pinned dependency versions, runs the test suite, and runs at least one static check (a linter, a type checker, or compiler warnings treated as errors, whichever your stack has) |
| FOSS | Upstream's CI passes on your pull request, and your fork documents the build-and-test command a maintainer expects you to have run before submitting |
| Research | The pipeline runs end to end on a small sample dataset in CI, with at least one test asserting a known result rather than the pipeline merely exiting zero |

## What You Submit

Read the path closest to your project in the [Shipping guide](/guides/shipping/) before you plan for the gates below.

Each checkpoint is one PDF, with the cover [Submitting Your Work](/assignments/introduction/#submitting-your-work) describes, that names the repository URL and the full hash of the commit you want graded. After the cover come the living docs at that commit, rendered, in this order: `docs/requirements.md`, `docs/design.md`, each decision record in `docs/rfcs/` or `docs/adr/`, `docs/risks.md`, `README.md`, `CONTRIBUTING.md`, and the AI context file. Your checkpoint's section below says what else the cover links and the PDF carries, and gives the gate as items you can check against your own repository at the named commit, each with the one place its evidence goes.

Push everything before you submit: the living docs in the PDF must match the named commit.

:::note[If your project is under NDA]
If your partner rules out read access for the instruction team, the checkpoint is a 15-minute screen-shared walkthrough instead of an asynchronous review, in a slot your team and your TA schedule in the checkpoint week, separate from your sprint demo. A designated student drives; the TA follows the same checklist against the repository at the named commit, in a fixed order (quickstart, CI, docs spot-check, risk register, PR list), and retains nothing beyond the PDF. Submit the PDF [sanitized](/introduction/ip-nda-and-licensing/#teams-under-an-nda) where your partner permits it; its living docs match the commit apart from what you redacted, and a redacted PR list and CI screenshots can support it. If your partner permits no copy, even sanitized, the PDF carries only the cover, the URL, and the hash, and the walkthrough stands in for the documents. In the walkthrough the TA checks each gate item the cover links (the CI run, the board, the deployment, the release) live, in the fixed order, so a link a grader cannot open does not fail the gate. The criteria and points are the same as for an asynchronous review. If even a walkthrough isn't possible, contact the instruction team.
:::

### Repo Checkpoint 1

Fall, week 5. The gate: your CI and board are in place, and the week 10 production target is agreed with your partner. The cover also links the CI run and the board. After the living docs, the PDF carries the inherited-codebase audit if your team starts in an existing codebase.

| Gate item | Met when, at the named commit | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| CI | Your CI, in the form [What CI Must Do](#what-ci-must-do) gives for your project, ran on the named commit and passed | A link on the cover to that run |
| Board | Every item in the current sprint has an owner and a status | A link on the cover to the board |
| Production target | One paragraph says where the [walking skeleton](https://wiki.c2.com/?WalkingSkeleton) will run in week 10, who owns the account or infrastructure, and who agreed it: your partner, or your mentor where you have no partner | `docs/design.md`, its deployment section |
| The target's approval | If the target needs an approval, an entry names it, who is asking for it, and when they asked | `docs/risks.md` |
| Inherited-codebase audit, if you start in an existing codebase | One page covering everything [the audit](#the-inherited-codebase-audit) below lists | A document in the PDF, after the living docs |

:::caution[Agree the production target in week 5, not week 9]
"Hello, production" at [Repo Checkpoint 2](#repo-checkpoint-2) is the one gate your team may not be able to meet alone: it can need an account, a hosting decision, a security review, or a maintainer's willingness to look at your pull request, and every one of those is somebody else's calendar. [Term Startup](/assignments/term-startup/#fall-by-sunday-of-week-2) has you raise the target with your partner by Sunday of fall week 2; this checkpoint asks for the agreed target and any approval it needs now, while there is time to chase them.

If the answer is "there will be no deployment", that's a fine answer, and it goes in the same paragraph of `docs/design.md` in week 5, so the instruction team can agree an equivalent gate while there is still time to hit it.
:::

#### The Inherited-Codebase Audit

Some teams don't start from scratch. Three origins, one situation: a predecessor capstone team's repository (read its `docs/risks.md` first, with the [handoff document](/assignments/project-handoff/) and the forward-looking section of the predecessor's project retrospective, which your partner holds), a project partner's codebase (their conventions, CI, review process, and hosting bind you from day one, which is the [RFC](/assignments/rfc/) page's "your partner already decided" case), or an upstream open-source project (their [CONTRIBUTING file](https://docs.github.com/en/communities/setting-up-your-project-for-healthy-contributions/setting-guidelines-for-repository-contributors), review culture, and release cadence bind you). In all three, someone else's decisions, tests, debt, and review process are yours before you write a line.

Audit what you inherited in fall weeks 1 to 5. This checkpoint checks it for every team that starts in an existing codebase, and it covers:

- what runs and how, and what does not;
- the state of the tests and the CI;
- the dependencies and their age;
- the open risks and the known debt;
- the predecessor team's handoff document and the forward-looking section of their project retrospective, if there is one;
- the upstream project's contribution rules, if there are any;
- what you will change first.

One page, submitted in this checkpoint's PDF after the living docs. What it finds that the code depends on goes into the repository where the next person looks: constraints in `docs/requirements.md`, conventions and pitfalls in the AI context file, and a decision to keep or replace something in an ADR. [Analyze an Existing Codebase](/activities/technical-design/#analyze-an-existing-codebase) is the exercise that produces it.

### Repo Checkpoint 2

Fall, week 10. The gate is "hello, production": a [walking skeleton](https://wiki.c2.com/?WalkingSkeleton), the thinnest end-to-end slice of your system that actually runs, deployed where Repo Checkpoint 1 recorded, plus a Definition of Shipped v0 draft. The cover also links the deployment. After the living docs, the PDF carries the [Definition of Shipped](/assignments/definition-of-shipped/) v0 draft.

| Gate item | Met when, at the named commit | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Walking skeleton deployed | Met in the shape the next table gives for your project, built from or submitted at the named commit | A link on the cover, to what the next table names |
| Definition of Shipped v0 | A draft of every item the Definition of Shipped asks for, whether or not your partner has agreed it yet | A document in the PDF, after the living docs |

| If your project is | Deployed means | The cover links |
|---|---|---|
| A web app or a hosted service | The skeleton serves requests in the production target | Its URL |
| A mobile app, game, desktop application, or firmware | A build of the skeleton installs from the channel `docs/design.md` names (a store test track, a release page, a device image) on a machine or device outside the team's own | The build's page on that channel |
| FOSS | Your first pull request to the upstream project is submitted | The pull request |
| Research | The pipeline runs end to end on sample data in CI | That CI run |
| Without a deployment, by agreement at Repo Checkpoint 1 | The equivalent gate `docs/design.md` records | The evidence recorded with that gate in `docs/design.md` |

### Repo Checkpoint 3

Winter, week 5. The gate is integration health: the stories planned for this term run on real components, your CI tests them, and a session with a real user is scheduled; `CONTRIBUTING.md` also shows the winter charter refresh. The cover also links the CI run.

| Gate item | Met when, at the named commit | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Integration | The stories `docs/requirements.md` marks for this term run end to end, against real components rather than stubs | `docs/requirements.md`, where each marked story says how to run it: the steps or the test |
| CI | The run on the named commit ran your tests and passed, and at least one test covers a story marked for this term | A link on the cover to that run |
| Real-user session scheduled | An entry gives the session's week, the participant's role (not their name), and who confirmed the session | `docs/risks.md`, as an external prerequisite |
| Charter refresh | The Definition of Done and the Contribution Norm reflect the winter charter refresh: changed since Repo Checkpoint 2, or marked as kept unchanged by the refresh | `CONTRIBUTING.md` in the PDF |

### Repo Checkpoint 4

Winter, week 10. The gate is a release candidate: someone outside the team can install or deploy it, and at least one session with a real user has run. The cover also links the release candidate. After the living docs, the PDF carries your real-user session notes.

| Gate item | Met when, at the named commit | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Release candidate | A release built from the named commit (a tagged version, a store build, or a release page) installs or deploys for someone outside the team, a grader included, using only `README.md` | A link on the cover to the release |
| Real-user session | Notes from at least one session: the participant's role (not their name), what they tried, what went wrong or confused them, and the issue or change each finding produced | A document in the PDF, after the living docs |

## Rubric

The four checkpoints share every criterion but Term gate, which grades each checkpoint's own gate. Each criterion has one thing graders check first, so you can check it yourself:

- **Living docs**: one recent feature, compared against `docs/design.md` in the PDF, one document compared against the named commit, and the Constraints section of `docs/requirements.md`. If the doc describes a system that no longer exists, or the PDF is not the commit, this criterion is where it shows.
- **Term gate**: judged against the gate table for this checkpoint under [What You Submit](#what-you-submit) and nothing else.
- **Build health**: the safety net is judged relative to how your team works, not against a fixed tool list. A team that delegates heavily to AI tools needs tighter automated gates than a team writing most code by hand, and neither approach is penalized: what is graded is whether the safety net catches what your way of working lets through. Gates belong on the changes that are expensive to undo (schema, authentication, deployment, migrations), not spread uniformly. A team with no CI that hand-merges agent output to production fails this regardless of whether anything has broken yet.
- **Decision and risk hygiene**: does the risk register have owners and a ranking, and is there exactly one of it?
- **Contribution traceability**: this grades whether the infrastructure for attribution exists, not whether every student contributed. Individual non-contribution is handled by the [individual contribution](/assignments/sprint-notes/#sprint-notes-n-individual-contribution) and never double-counted here.

What "a history a maintainer could inherit" means is spelled out in the [git and GitHub guide](/guides/git-and-github/#a-history-a-maintainer-could-inherit); the checkpoint checks the five items listed there.

## Activities That Prepare This

Checkpoints happen four times across fall and winter, and each gate needs different preparation, so the recommendations are grouped by checkpoint rather than offered as one list.

**Repo Checkpoint 1, fall week 5:** [Analyze an Existing Codebase](/activities/technical-design/#analyze-an-existing-codebase) for the inherited-codebase audit if you start in someone else's code; [Describe your Architecture](/activities/technical-design/#describe-your-architecture) and [User Story Mapping](/activities/requirements/#user-story-mapping) for the living docs; [Kanban Board Setup](/activities/team-and-workflow/#kanban-board-setup) and [Definition of Done](/activities/team-and-workflow/#definition-of-done) once, for the gate and build health; [Audit Your Safety Net](/activities/testing-release-and-handoff/#audit-your-safety-net) for the safety-net half; [Risk Management Plan](/activities/planning-and-risk/#risk-management-plan) for the first entries in the risk register.

**Repo Checkpoint 2, fall week 10, "hello, production":** [Deployment Plan](/activities/testing-release-and-handoff/#deployment-plan), [Plan Prototype](/activities/planning-and-risk/#plan-prototype), and [Software Release and Versioning](/activities/testing-release-and-handoff/#software-release-and-versioning) for the gate; [Put a Browser Agent on Your Critical Flow](/activities/testing-release-and-handoff/#put-a-browser-agent-on-your-critical-flow) for build health if your product runs in a browser.

**Repo Checkpoint 3, winter week 5, integration health:** [Test Plan](/activities/testing-release-and-handoff/#test-plan) for build health, and [Wire an Accessibility Audit Into CI](/activities/interaction-design/#wire-an-accessibility-audit-into-ci) too if your product runs in a browser; [Find Users](/activities/working-with-users/#find-users) then [Engage Users](/activities/working-with-users/#engage-users) for the scheduled real-user session.

**Repo Checkpoint 4, winter week 10, release candidate:** [Run an Acceptance Pass](/activities/testing-release-and-handoff/#run-an-acceptance-pass) for the real-user evidence, and [Test with an Assistive Technology User](/activities/interaction-design/#test-with-an-assistive-technology-user) too if your product has a user interface; [Describe your API Reference](/activities/testing-release-and-handoff/#describe-your-api-reference) for the living docs if other code or people build on your product; [Fishbone Diagram](/activities/planning-and-risk/#fishbone-diagram) for risk hygiene.
