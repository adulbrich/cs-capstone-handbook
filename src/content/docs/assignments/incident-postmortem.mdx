By week 9 of winter, something real has gone wrong: an outage, a bad merge that cost a weekend, a dependency or platform surprise, a security scare, a missed commitment, a partner miscommunication that burned a sprint. Pick the most instructive one and write the document engineering teams write when it happens to them: a **blameless postmortem**.

Blameless means the unit of analysis is the system, not the person. "Alex broke the deploy" is banned; "the deploy process allowed an untested migration to reach production because nothing checked for it" is the genre. The test of blamelessness: every person involved would happily co-sign the document. [Google's SRE book chapter on postmortem culture](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/) is the reference text for the practice, and the Retrospectives guide covers [what a blameless postmortem costs when it is not](/guides/retrospectives/#psychological-safety-first). **Write to the six sections below**, which are what the rubric checks: the guide's [five-area sketch](/guides/retrospectives/#postmortem-format) is the industry shape, not this assignment's.

:::note[If nothing went wrong]
Some terms really are quiet. If your team cannot name an incident, write the postmortem on a **near miss**: something that would have caused real damage and did not, because a test failed, a review comment landed, someone noticed in time, or you got lucky. [Near-miss analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_miss_(safety)) is standard practice in aviation, medicine and industrial safety, where waiting for real incidents is too expensive to be a learning strategy. The document is identical except that the impact section becomes what the impact would have been and how close it came.

If you can't name a near miss either, that's your finding, and it's a real one: nothing in your process is currently surfacing problems early enough to see them. Write that up instead, using the same structure, with the timeline tracing how a plausible failure would travel through your system undetected and the corrective actions aimed at detection. Tell your Teaching Assistant (TA) in week 7 rather than week 9, so there's time to scope it properly.
:::

## Structure (2 to 3 pages)

Where the incident is technical, keep the postmortem with the work, in the repository: an issue, an ADR, or the PR that fixes it. Otherwise keep it with [your team's documents](/assignments/repo-checkpoints/#what-the-repository-must-contain-the-living-docs).

1. **Summary and impact**: what happened, when, who and what was affected, in factual, measurable terms.
2. **Timeline**: the sequence of events, decisions, and signals (including missed ones), timestamped where possible.
3. **Root cause analysis**: why it happened, past the first answer ([five whys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_whys) or contributing-factors style). Include what made detection slow, if it was.
4. **What went well**: what limited the damage; credit where it's due.
5. **Corrective actions**: 3 to 5 specific changes with owners and dates, distinguishing "prevent recurrence" from "detect faster". At least one must be verifiable at the [week-10 repo checkpoint](/assignments/repo-checkpoints/) or in the [week-10 sprint note](/assignments/sprint-notes/).
6. **AI involvement, if any**: if AI-generated code or advice contributed to the incident or to the recovery, say so plainly; both are normal and instructive.

Start from the [postmortem template](/postmortem-template.md).

**AI use:** AI may reconstruct the timeline from logs, commits, and chat history, and draft the write-up. It must not invent events or timestamps, or supply a tidier root cause than the evidence supports. A fabricated timeline, or a corrective action with no real owner, fails the assignment. Section 6 asks you to say when AI contributed to the incident; the same disclosure applies to the document.
