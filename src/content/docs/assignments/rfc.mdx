**Individual submission. Fall and winter terms. 15% of the term grade.**

An RFC (Request for Comments) is how engineering organizations make significant decisions in writing: someone frames the problem, lays out the options, recommends one, and invites critique before the team commits. You will write one RFC per term (fall and winter) about a **real decision your team currently faces**. Not a hypothetical, not a retrospective justification of something already built: a live decision where your document should genuinely change what the team does.

Writing a precise, well-scoped RFC is the engineering skill whose value has gone up the most with AI tools: the quality of what you can direct an agent or a teammate to build is bounded by the quality of your specification.

## The Cycle

| Step | When | What |
|---|---|---|
| Draft | End of week 4 | 800 to 1,500 words, committed to your repo under `docs/rfcs/` (Track B teams submit a PDF export) |
| Cross-team feedback | Week 5 | You give structured feedback on two drafts from **other teams**; two students review yours |
| Revision and decision | End of week 8 | Revised RFC plus a short response-to-feedback note; your team records the decision (accepted, rejected, superseded) |

Feedback is structured: for each draft you review, answer four questions in writing (Is the problem framed so a newcomer understands the stakes? Is any credible option missing? Would the verification plan actually detect failure? What is the single strongest improvement?). Reviewing other teams' thinking is not overhead; it is the cheapest way to see how a different project reasons.

## What the RFC Must Contain

1. **Problem and context**: what decision is needed, why now, what happens if the team does nothing.
2. **Options**: at least three credible options including "do nothing", each with honest costs.
3. **Analysis**: the technical tradeoffs that matter (performance, complexity, maintainability, security, cost), argued with evidence, not vibes.
4. **Recommendation and verification plan**: what you propose and, concretely, how the team will know it worked (tests, metrics, acceptance criteria).
5. **Constraints**: licensing, data use, IP, and the partner confidentiality boundary as they bear on this decision.
6. **AI disclosure**: which tools you used, for what, and what verification you performed on their output.

## Rubric (100 points)

| Criterion | Points | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Problem framing and context: a newcomer understands the decision and the stakes | 15 | SO1 |
| Options: three or more credible options including "do nothing", with honest costs | 10 | SO1 |
| Technical tradeoff analysis: evidence-backed, project-specific, acknowledges uncertainty | 15 | SO6 |
| Recommendation with a concrete verification plan | 15 | SO2 |
| Constraints: licensing, data use, IP, confidentiality boundary addressed | 10 | SO4 |
| AI disclosure: tools, purposes, and verification performed | 10 | SO4 |
| Writing: structured, professional, right for a technical audience | 10 | SO3, L08 |
| Revision: substantive response to feedback, with a response note | 10 | SO3, L09 |
| Feedback given: your written reviews of two peer drafts are specific and useful | 5 | SO3, L07 |

Non-submission of the draft forfeits the revision points (there is nothing to revise) in addition to the draft's own late handling; non-submission of peer feedback forfeits those 5 points and is visible to the affected teams.

:::note[Relationship to ADRs]
When your team records the decision, the RFC becomes the Architecture Decision Record: add the outcome and date at the top. See the [ADR guide](/guides/adr/). One document, two lifecycle stages.
:::
