An RFC (Request for Comments) is how engineering organizations make significant decisions in writing: someone frames the problem, lays out the options, recommends one, and invites critique before the team commits. The name comes from the [IETF's RFC series](https://www.ietf.org/process/rfcs/), which is how the internet's protocols are still specified; the same shape is used internally at Google as a [design doc](https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/) and at Oxide as an [RFD](https://rfd.shared.oxide.computer/rfd/0001). You write a different one each term, in fall and winter, about a **real decision your team faces that is hard to reverse**. In fall the decision is still open. In winter it may be one the team has already started acting on, as long as the RFC still changes something: how the team verifies it, what reversing it would take, or what comes next. Not a hypothetical, and not a justification written after the fact for a decision the RFC no longer changes.

:::note[Picking a decision that is actually yours]
Hard-to-reverse decisions are typically architecture, database schema, authentication and authorization, deployment topology, data migrations, licensing, and third-party commitments: undoing them costs far more than getting them right now. Most feature code is easy to reverse. The distinction comes from Jeff Bezos's [2015 letter to Amazon shareholders](https://s2.q4cdn.com/299287126/files/doc_financials/annual/2015-Letter-to-Shareholders.PDF), which calls them Type 1 and Type 2 decisions and warns that applying the heavyweight Type 1 process to reversible decisions makes an organization slow. Telling one from the other is itself part of the exercise, and it's the judgment that matters most when much of the building is delegated.

Two situations make the choice harder, and both are ordinary:

Your partner already decided. Your team may inherit the stack, the hosting, the database, and the review process, and find out in week 3 how little is yours to choose. Write on a decision inside that constrained space, or on how to work within a constraint you were handed: how to verify against a system you cannot change, how to migrate data on a schedule someone else owns, what you would have to prove before asking your partner to reconsider. Naming a constraint accurately and reasoning inside it is a legitimate RFC topic.

Nothing hard to reverse is live in your four-week window. Write on the most consequential reversible decision you face, and say in the problem framing why it is reversible and what would make it not.

Do not invent a hard-to-reverse decision you are not actually facing.
:::

What you can direct an agent or a teammate to build is bounded by how precisely you can specify it. Write the RFC to that standard.

## What the RFC Must Contain

1. **Problem and context**: what decision is needed, why now, what happens if the team does nothing.
2. **Options**: at least three credible options including "do nothing", each with its real costs.
3. **Analysis**: the technical tradeoffs that matter (performance, complexity, maintainability, security, cost), argued from cited sources and your own project evidence.
4. **Recommendation and verification plan**: what you propose and, concretely, how the team will know it worked (tests, metrics, acceptance criteria).
5. **Constraints**: licensing, data use, IP, and the partner confidentiality boundary as they bear on this decision.
6. **Delegation and validation**: what you handed to AI tools on this decision, what checks made that reasonable (tests, CI, review gates, staging, a rollback path), and the specific points where you stopped and validated the result yourself. The [AI policy](/assignments/introduction/#ai-policy-applies-to-every-assignment) says what is assessed.

Write it in the format the [ADR guide](/guides/adr/) sets out, starting from the [RFC template](/rfc-template.md), and commit it to your repository under `docs/rfcs/` or `docs/adr/`. Both entries below take it as one PDF ([Submitting Your Work](/assignments/introduction/#submitting-your-work)). The length is 800 to 1,500 words in fall and 1,500 to 2,500 in winter, with a 2,000-word floor on the winter final (see [RFC Final Draft](#rfc-final-draft)).

**AI use:** AI may research options, draft sections, and sharpen the argument; the Delegation and validation criterion is where you say what you handed off and how you checked it. Presenting a generated analysis you did not verify as your own judgment, or inventing evidence (benchmarks, quotes, references that do not exist), fails the assignment. The defense will ask you to explain the decision without the tool.

## RFC Draft + Peer Review

<AssignmentMeta submission="Individual" due="Draft end of week 4; peer reviews end of week 5" weight="5% of the term grade" />

### What You Submit

Submit a complete draft by the end of week 4: every section above, within the term's length band. The draft is graded for completeness, not for the quality of its reasoning. You don't need its grade before revising: start as soon as your peer reviews arrive.

### The Peer Review

In week 5 you review two drafts by other students, and two students review yours. Your reviews carry most of this entry's points, and they are the part graded on quality.

1. **Get your pairings.** At the start of week 5, you are assigned two drafts through the peer review on this entry.
2. **Answer four questions for each draft**, in writing:
   1. Is the problem framed so a newcomer understands the stakes?
   2. Is any credible option missing?
   3. Would the verification plan actually detect failure?
   4. What is the single strongest improvement?
3. **Point at the draft.** Each answer names the passage it is about, so the author can find it.
4. **Submit both reviews** by the end of week 5, as a comment on each draft in its peer review.

If an assigned reviewer does not submit, flag it to your Teaching Assistant (TA): you won't be penalized for feedback you never received, and the instruction team will supply a review. Not submitting your own reviews forfeits the review criteria below and is visible to the authors you were paired with.

:::note[If your project is under NDA]
Submit a sanitized draft: your reviewers read it, so replace partner-confidential specifics with neutral placeholders. If that isn't possible, contact the instruction team.
:::

## RFC Final Draft

<AssignmentMeta submission="Individual" due="End of week 8" weight="10% of the term grade" />

### What You Submit

Submit the revised RFC by the end of week 8, with a short response-to-feedback note saying what you changed, what you declined, and why. Your team records the decision in the RFC (accepted, rejected, or superseded). The instruction team grades the final against the rubric below and returns rubric feedback. Not submitting the draft forfeits the revision points, because there is nothing to revise. The standard [late policy](/assignments/introduction/#late-work-absence-and-makeup) applies to both entries.

:::note[Winter length floor]
CS 462 is the writing intensive term: the winter RFC's revised version, including the response-to-feedback note, must total **at least 2,000 words**.
