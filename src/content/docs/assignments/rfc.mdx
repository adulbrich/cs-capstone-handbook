You write a different RFC each term, in fall and winter, about a **real decision on your project that is [hard to reverse](/guides/generative-ai/#verify-what-matters-and-build-the-checks-that-let-you-trust-the-rest)**. An RFC frames the problem, lays out the options, recommends one, and invites critique; the name comes from the [Internet Engineering Task Force's RFC series](https://www.ietf.org/process/rfcs/), and Google uses the same shape internally as a [design doc](https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/), Oxide as an [RFD (Request for Discussion)](https://rfd.shared.oxide.computer/rfd/0001). The decision may be open or already made at either submission, and the RFC says where it stands: proposed, accepted, rejected, or superseded. Your team decides when the work needs it, not when the RFC is due. The RFC is not a hypothetical.

:::note[If your partner already decided]
You may inherit the stack, hosting, database, and review process, and learn in week 3 how little is yours to choose. Write on one of them, decisions inside them, or working within one: how to verify against a system you cannot change, migrate data on a schedule someone else owns, or what to prove before asking your partner to reconsider.
:::

:::note[If your project has no hard-to-reverse decision]
Write on the most consequential reversible decision you face, and say in the problem framing why it is reversible and what would make it not. Don't invent a hard-to-reverse decision you aren't actually facing.
:::

What you can direct an agent or a teammate to build is bounded by how precisely you can specify it. Write the RFC to that standard.

## What You Produce

1. **Problem and context**: what decision is needed, why now, what happens if the team does nothing.
2. **Options**: at least three options including "do nothing", each with its real costs and the condition under which it would be the right choice.
3. **Analysis**: the technical tradeoffs that matter (performance, complexity, maintainability, security, cost), argued from cited sources and your own project evidence.
4. **Recommendation and verification plan**: the option the analysis supports, even if your team chose another, and how the team knows or will know the decision worked (tests, metrics, acceptance criteria).
5. **Constraints**: licensing, data use, IP, or confidentiality limits this decision must respect, and what each changes.
6. **Delegation and validation**: what you handed to AI tools on this decision, what checks made that reasonable (tests, CI, review gates, staging, a rollback path), and the specific points where you stopped and validated the result yourself. The [AI policy](/assignments/introduction/#ai-policy-applies-to-every-assignment) says what is assessed.

Write it in the sections of the [RFC template](/rfc-template.md): the six above, then Decision. Commit the draft to your repository under `docs/rfcs/` or `docs/adr/` when you submit it, with Status and Decision showing where the decision stands; update both when it changes, and keep the file as the [decision record](/guides/adr/) from then on. You submit the RFC twice, each time as one PDF ([Submitting Your Work](/assignments/introduction/#submitting-your-work)): the draft in week 4 and the revised final draft in week 8. The draft is 1,500 to 2,500 words and the final draft 2,000 to 2,500, in fall and in winter, not counting the revision log (see [RFC Final Draft](#rfc-final-draft)).

**AI use:** AI may research options, draft sections, and sharpen the argument; the Delegation and validation criterion is where you say what you handed off and how you checked it. Presenting a generated analysis you did not verify as your own judgment, or inventing evidence (benchmarks, quotes, references that do not exist), fails the assignment.

## What You Submit

### RFC Draft + Peer Review

Submit a complete draft by Sunday of week 4: every section above, 1,500 to 2,500 words. [You can build your submission PDF from your Markdown files.](/tools/submission-pdf/) You don't need the draft's grade before revising: start as soon as your peer reviews arrive.

#### The Peer Review

In week 5 you review two drafts by other students, and two students review yours. Your reviews carry most of this entry's points, and the larger share goes to how specific each one is to its draft.

1. **Get your pairings.** At the start of week 5, you are assigned two drafts through the peer review on this entry.
2. **Answer four questions for each draft**, in writing:
   1. Is the problem framed so a newcomer understands the stakes?
   2. Is any credible option missing?
   3. Would the verification plan actually detect failure?
   4. What is the single strongest improvement?
3. **Point at the draft.** Each answer names the passage it is about, so the author can find it.
4. **Submit both reviews** by Sunday of week 5, as a comment on each draft in its peer review.

If an assigned reviewer does not submit, flag it to your TA: you won't be penalized for feedback you never received, and the instruction team will supply a review. Not submitting your reviews of other students' drafts forfeits the review criteria below and is visible to the authors you were paired with.

:::note[If your project is under NDA]
Submit a sanitized draft: your reviewers read it, so replace partner-confidential specifics with neutral placeholders. If that isn't possible, contact the instruction team.
:::

#### Instructor Comments

By Sunday of week 6, an instructor returns comments on your draft submission. Each comment becomes a row in your [revision log](#revision-log), the same as a point from a peer review.

### RFC Final Draft

Submit the RFC by Sunday of week 8, revised after both peer reviews and the instructor comments, followed by its revision log, in one PDF. [You can build your submission PDF from your Markdown files.](/tools/submission-pdf/) Bring its Status and Decision up to date first, and commit the revised RFC in place of the draft. The instruction team grades the final draft against the rubric below and returns rubric feedback.

#### Revision Log

After the RFC, add a table with one row for every point raised in the feedback you received: both peer reviews (one may be a review the instruction team supplied in place of a reviewer who did not submit) and the instructor comments on your draft. Each row records what you did with that point:

| Source | Feedback | Decision | Where or why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reviewer 1 | No check in the verification plan would catch a migration that drops rows | Changed | Recommendation and Verification Plan: a row-count and checksum comparison now runs before cutover |
| Reviewer 2 | A managed queue is a missing option | Declined | The partner's hosting agreement rules out a third-party processor for this data, so a managed queue is not a credible option |

Feedback is the point in your words, not a quote. For a change, Where or why names the section that moved and what moved in it, so the grader can find it in the final draft. For a decline, it gives the reason the point does not hold for this decision; a reasoned decline counts as much as a change.

The log goes in the submission only: the RFC in your repository is the team's record of the decision, and who asked for which change is not part of it, so don't commit the log there. It does not count toward the length band.
