An RFC (Request for Comments) is how engineering organizations make significant decisions in writing: someone frames the problem, lays out the options, recommends one, and invites critique before the team commits. The name comes from the [IETF's RFC series](https://www.ietf.org/process/rfcs/), which is how the internet's protocols are still specified; the same shape is used internally at Google as a [design doc](https://www.industrialempathy.com/posts/design-docs-at-google/) and at Oxide as an [RFD](https://rfd.shared.oxide.computer/rfd/0001). You write one per term, in fall and winter, about a **real decision your team currently faces that is hard to reverse**. Not a hypothetical and not a retrospective justification of something already built: a live decision where your document should change what the team does.

:::note[Picking a decision that is actually yours]
Hard-to-reverse decisions are typically architecture, database schema, authentication and authorization, deployment topology, data migrations, licensing, and third-party commitments: undoing them costs far more than getting them right now. Most feature code is easy to reverse. The distinction comes from Jeff Bezos's [2015 letter to Amazon shareholders](https://s2.q4cdn.com/299287126/files/doc_financials/annual/2015-Letter-to-Shareholders.PDF), which calls them Type 1 and Type 2 decisions and warns that applying the heavyweight Type 1 process to reversible decisions makes an organization slow. Telling one from the other is itself part of the exercise, and it's the judgment that matters most when much of the building is delegated.

Two situations make the choice harder, and both are ordinary:

Your partner already decided. Your team may inherit the stack, the hosting, the database, and the review process, and find out in week 3 how little is yours to choose. Write on a decision inside that constrained space, or on how to work within a constraint you were handed: how to verify against a system you cannot change, how to migrate data on a schedule someone else owns, what you would have to prove before asking your partner to reconsider. Naming a constraint accurately and reasoning inside it is a legitimate RFC topic.

Nothing hard to reverse is live in your four-week window. Write on the most consequential reversible decision you face, and say in the problem framing why it is reversible and what would make it not.

Do not invent a hard-to-reverse decision you are not actually facing.
:::

What you can direct an agent or a teammate to build is bounded by how precisely you can specify it. Write the RFC to that standard.

## The Cycle

| Step | When | What |
|---|---|---|
| Draft | End of week 4 | 800 to 1,500 words in fall; 1,500 to 2,500 in winter (see note below); committed to your repo under `docs/rfcs/` or `docs/adr/`, in the format the [ADR guide](/guides/adr/) sets out, and submitted in Canvas as one PDF with your name and email on the cover |
| Cross-team feedback | Week 5 | You give structured feedback on two drafts from other teams (the instruction team generates the pairings and publishes them in Canvas at the start of week 5); two students review yours |
| Revision and decision | End of week 8 | Revised RFC plus a short response-to-feedback note; your team records the decision (accepted, rejected, superseded) |

Start from the [RFC template](/rfc-template.md).

:::note[NDA teams and cross-team review]
If your project is under NDA, do not hand reviewers partner-confidential context. Write the review copy of your RFC as a **decision skeleton**: keep the decision structure, options, tradeoff reasoning, and verification plan, and replace confidential specifics with neutral placeholders ("the partner's internal system", "the dataset"). If even that is not possible under your agreement, tell your Teaching Assistant (TA) by week 4 and the instruction team will review your draft instead. Your AI Coordinator's confidentiality one-pager governs what may leave the boundary.
:::

Feedback is structured: for each draft you review, answer four questions in writing. Is the problem framed so a newcomer understands the stakes? Is any credible option missing? Would the verification plan actually detect failure? What is the single strongest improvement?

## What the RFC Must Contain

1. **Problem and context**: what decision is needed, why now, what happens if the team does nothing.
2. **Options**: at least three credible options including "do nothing", each with its real costs.
3. **Analysis**: the technical tradeoffs that matter (performance, complexity, maintainability, security, cost), argued from cited sources and your own project evidence.
4. **Recommendation and verification plan**: what you propose and, concretely, how the team will know it worked (tests, metrics, acceptance criteria).
5. **Constraints**: licensing, data use, IP, and the partner confidentiality boundary as they bear on this decision.
6. **Delegation and validation**: what you handed to AI tools on this decision, what checks made that reasonable (tests, CI, review gates, staging, a rollback path), and the specific points where you stopped and validated the result yourself. The [AI policy](/assignments/introduction/#ai-policy-applies-to-every-assignment) says what is assessed.

Non-submission of the draft forfeits the revision points (there is nothing to revise); non-submission of your peer reviews forfeits the review criteria and is visible to the affected teams. If your assigned reviewer no-shows, flag it to your TA: you will not be penalized for feedback you never received, and the instruction team will supply a review. The standard [late policy](/assignments/introduction/#late-work-absence-and-makeup) applies.

Expect written margin feedback on the draft from your two peer reviewers and rubric feedback on the final from the instruction team.

**AI use:** AI may research options, draft sections, and sharpen the argument; the Delegation and validation criterion is where you say what you handed off and how you checked it. Presenting a generated analysis you did not verify as your own judgment, or inventing evidence (benchmarks, quotes, references that do not exist), fails the assignment. The defense will ask you to explain the decision without the tool.

:::note[Winter length floor]
CS 462 is the writing intensive term: the winter RFC's revised version, including the response-to-feedback note, must total **at least 2,000 words**.
:::
