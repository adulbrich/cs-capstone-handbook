Once per term, in week 7 or 9 (check Canvas for the schedule), your team meets a Teaching Assistant (TA) or instructor who is not your team's regular TA for a defense session separate from your [TA check-ins](/assignments/introduction/#ta-check-ins), during which each student walks through something they own and answers live questions. Your own TA does not attend, so the account you give of your work has to stand on its own. The session is six minutes per student plus about ten minutes for setup and wrap, so a team of three runs a little under half an hour and a team of five runs about forty minutes. Per-student time doesn't shrink on a larger team; the session simply runs longer. Sick or absent that week? Tell your TA before the session and you will be rescheduled, per the [absence policy](/assignments/introduction/#late-work-absence-and-makeup).

You may present a pull request you drove, a subsystem you maintain, your RFC decision, or a test or deployment strategy you built. Expect questions like these: why this way and what you rejected, what breaks if X changes, where did you decide to stop and check the work yourself and why there, and what was the most useful thing you told someone whose work you reviewed.

**Ground rules:** your docs, your repo, and your AI tools are open for explaining (looking things up, walking through code) and closed for generating (no asking a model to produce the answer live). Here the [AI policy](/assignments/introduction/#ai-policy-applies-to-every-assignment) narrows: what is measured is what you can account for without help. Notes are fine. Slides are unnecessary.

If you built and understood your work, the defense is a friendly conversation about it. If you can't explain your own contribution, no artifact will cover for that, and your project partner will expect the same of you in every meeting.

Delegating work to AI tools is expected and is not penalized; the [AI policy](/assignments/introduction/#ai-policy-applies-to-every-assignment) says what is assessed instead. In a defense that's concrete: a student who let an agent drive a database migration unchecked and got lucky scores badly, and a student who stopped to validate an authentication change scores well even if some feature code has bugs.

## How the Session Runs

The speaking order is drawn at random in the room, never published in advance, and each student gets a different artifact and a different "what breaks if X" question. Hearing a teammate answer first therefore tells you very little about what you will be asked.

Your teammates are the people who know whether your account of your own work is true, so claim only what they would co-sign.

If a score comes out borderline, or if your part of the session ran short for any reason, your assessor may ask you back for a brief individual follow-up. It is routine and it is not a penalty.

## Scoring

TAs and instructors score each student live on a printed sheet (the [scoresheet](/defense-scoresheet.md) is public: you can see exactly what is scored). Scores and notes are entered per student into Canvas after the session; the printed sheet is the live instrument, Canvas is the record. No written deliverable exists; there's nothing to prepare beyond doing your work and being able to talk about it. Not every teammate holds one of the three named roles (Project Manager, AI Coordinator, Quality Owner) at a given time; "comparable ownership area" (a subsystem, the test suite, the deployment) satisfies the role criterion. The [working agreement guide](/guides/working-agreement/#roles-and-ownership) says what each of the three concerns covers. On a small team a student may own two of the three concerns, and speaks to both.

## Rubric (100 points, per student)

"Handles not-knowing gracefully" is real: "I don't know, but here is how I would find out" scores; bluffing does not.

Communication runs both directions, and the reviewer-side half checks the other direction: being articulate about your own work doesn't make you useful in review. In fall and winter it's your [RFC](/assignments/rfc/) cross-team review; in spring it's code review, and your [sprint note](/assignments/sprint-notes/) contribution lines already name the one change you asked for. Both are graded records, so this is checkable: "I left some comments" is not an answer, and neither is anything that named no line and requested no change. [Google's code review standard](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/standard.html) is the bar the criterion is written against.

:::note[NDA teams]
The defense runs identically whether or not your project is under NDA: everything happens live and no artifact changes hands, so NDA constraints do not apply.
:::
