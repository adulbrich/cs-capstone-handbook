Once per term, in week 7 or 9 (check announcements for the schedule), your team meets a TA or instructor who is not your team's regular TA for a defense session separate from your [TA check-ins](/assignments/introduction/#ta-check-ins), during which each student walks through something they own and answers live questions. Your own TA does not attend, so the account you give of your work has to stand on its own.

Bring two or three things you own, such as a PR you drove, a subsystem you maintain, an experiment or analysis you ran, your RFC decision, or a test or deployment strategy you built; the assessor picks one, a different one for each student. Prepare each the way a behavioral interview question asks for it: the situation, what you did and what you rejected, what you handed to AI tools, the result, and what you would do differently now. Expect questions like these: why this way and what you rejected, what breaks if X changes, where you stopped to check the work yourself and why there, and what was the most useful thing you told someone whose work you reviewed.

Delegating to AI tools is expected; the [AI policy](/assignments/introduction/#ai-policy-applies-to-every-assignment) says what is assessed instead. Say what you handed to AI tools and how it went, including what went wrong. A student who names a delegation that went wrong, the check it lacked, and the check now in their sprint notes or repository scores well, as does one who stopped to validate an authentication change even if some feature code has bugs.

## How the Session Runs

The session is six minutes per student plus about ten minutes for setup and wrap, so a team of three runs a little under half an hour and a team of five about forty minutes.

**Ground rules:** your docs, your repo, and your AI tools are open for explaining (looking things up, walking through code) and closed for generating (no asking a model to produce the answer live). Notes are fine. Slides are unnecessary.

The speaking order is drawn at random in the room, never published in advance, and each student gets a different artifact and a different "what breaks if X" question. Hearing a teammate answer first therefore tells you very little about what you will be asked.

Your teammates are the people who know whether your account of your own work is true, so claim only what they would co-sign.

If a score comes out borderline, or if your part of the session ran short for any reason, your assessor may ask you back for a brief individual follow-up. It is routine and it is not a penalty.

:::note[If your project is under NDA]
Show your work on a shared screen, within what your partner allows, with partner-confidential specifics [described in neutral terms](/introduction/ip-nda-and-licensing/#teams-under-an-nda). The assessor checks your claims against what you show rather than the repository. If that isn't possible, contact the instruction team.
:::

## Scoring

TAs and instructors score each student live on a printed sheet (the [scoresheet](/defense-scoresheet.md) is public: you can see exactly what is scored). The role criterion asks what your role (Project Manager, AI Coordinator, or Quality Owner) or the ownership area your [team charter](/assignments/team-charter/#1-roles) gives you required this term, how the team made decisions and split the work, and which decision you chaired, meaning drove to a conclusion, or are scheduled to chair under the charter's rotation. If you hold two of the three roles, speak to both.

## What You Submit

Nothing. The defense is live, so prepare by doing your work and being able to talk about it.

## Rubric

The second half of the communication criterion is about review: what you told someone whose work you reviewed, and what changed because of it. In fall and winter it's your [RFC](/assignments/rfc/) peer review, and since the RFC you reviewed may not be revised yet, the author's reply to your comment, or the revision they committed to, counts as what changed. In spring it's the reviews you did on your team's work, and the record is the reviewer notes you already wrote in your [sprint notes](/assignments/sprint-notes/#what-got-done-and-who) on the reviews that took judgment. [Google's code review standard](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/standard.html) is the bar the criterion is written against.
