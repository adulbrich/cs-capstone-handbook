At the end of every two-week [sprint](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html), the team submits a one-page sprint note, as one PDF ([Submitting Your Work](/assignments/introduction/#submitting-your-work)), and demos working software. The note is evidence plus judgment, not prose: if your repository and board are in order, producing it takes 15 minutes. The [sprint guide](/guides/sprint/) covers how to plan, run, and review a sprint; the windows below are when this course's sprints fall.

| Term | Sprint windows |
|---|---|
| Fall | Weeks 3 to 4, 5 to 6, 7 to 8, 9 to 10 |
| Winter | Weeks 1 to 2, 3 to 4, 5 to 6, 7 to 8, 9 to 10 |
| Spring | Weeks 1 to 2, 3 to 4, 5 to 6 |

**AI use:** you may generate the evidence with AI from your own repo history and board. Two things must be human-written, because they are the parts anyone reads: the judgment layer, and the one sentence in each contribution line saying what you shipped and how you know it works. Start from the [sprint note template](/sprint-note-template.md).

## The Sprint Note (one page, five items)

1. **Working software evidence**: a URL, tagged release, or demo video, plus the CI run. If nothing works yet, say so plainly and say why.
2. **What got done**: the things the team actually finished this sprint, each one concrete and consequential. Link the board.
3. **Top risk and one decision needed**: the single biggest threat to shipping (in fall, before your [Definition of Shipped](/assignments/definition-of-shipped/) exists, the biggest threat to the term gate), and one concrete decision the team needs from the partner, the instruction team, or itself.
4. **Contribution lines**: one per student, in the shape below.
5. **Judgment layer** (about five lines, human-written): what the team learned this sprint and what it changes about the plan.

### What counts as "done"

Concrete means someone could go and look at it. Consequential means the project is meaningfully further along because of it. It does not have to be code. A partner agreement reached, a dataset cleared for use, a deployment pipeline that now works, a real-user session run and written up, a flaky test suite made reliable: all of these are done things worth naming. Work that is not code counts when it is measurable, which means it landed in the repository as a written PR someone can open: findings, interview notes, a requirements section, an RFC section.

What does not count is activity without a result. "Worked on the API", "researched options", "had meetings", and "made progress on the frontend" describe how time was spent, not what exists now that did not exist two weeks ago.

Everything in this list should be something you can show. The demo and this list are the same content in two formats.

### Contribution lines

Every student owns at least one reviewed pull request per sprint. That is the contribution floor your team agrees in its [Team Charter](/assignments/team-charter/), and [Individual Accountability on Team Work](/assignments/introduction/#individual-accountability-on-team-work) says how the evidence for it is read.

One line per student, three parts:

1. **The pull request(s) you owned this sprint**, by title or ID. NDA teams list IDs only. Owning it means you authored or drove it, answered review, and merged it. A written artifact counts when it landed as a PR like anything else; a rename, a formatter run, a lockfile bump, or a status update does not.
2. **Whose work you reviewed, and the one change you asked for.** Name it specifically: "asked Priya to handle the empty-cart case in checkout" is a review; "LGTM" is not, and neither is a comment that names no line and requests no change.
3. **The most consequential thing you shipped this sprint, in one sentence, and how the team knows it works.** This is the part with judgment in it, and it's the part that's read.

[Google's code review standard](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/standard.html) is worth reading on where that bar sits: approve once the change improves the overall code health of the system, even when it is not perfect, and say what would need to change if it does not.

Also name **who chaired a decision** this sprint and which decision they drove to a conclusion. Over a term every student should appear here at least once. This is the record your [defense](/assignments/defense/) role criterion is checked against, so it saves you reconstructing your own leadership from memory in week 8.

:::note[How the numbers are read]
Pull request and review counts are evidence where "everyone contributed" is not. Volume is easy to inflate and easy to misread, so three rules bound it.

Mechanical changes are not evidence. A formatter run, a lockfile bump, a bulk rename, a generated file, a dependency update: all normal work, none of it a signal about contribution. Do not pad your line with them, and graders will not count them. Lines of code are not counted either: a lint pass can move ten thousand lines and mean nothing.

The numbers are only ever read as a pattern. One sprint where you shipped little, with an explanation, is ordinary life. Four sprints of nothing with no explanation is what costs you points. No single sprint's count decides anything.

The sentence outranks the counts. A student with one pull request who can say what it made possible and how they know it works is contributing more than a student with six and nothing to say about any of them.
:::

## Demo Cadence

Every sprint, your team demos live to your Teaching Assistant (TA) in a check-in during the sprint-note week, in person or over Zoom, at a time your team and your TA agree. One team, one TA, nobody else's team in the room. Every student demos their own work: pick one artifact from your contribution line for this sprint (the PR or the document you listed as owned), open it, show the change, and run it. It cannot be the artifact you showed at the last check-in: nothing new to show means nothing was done that sprint. Your TA follows up with a question about what you showed, such as which lines are yours or what your reviewer asked you to change.

Two to three minutes each, so 6 to 12 minutes of a 25-to-30-minute check-in for a team of three or four. The rest of the check-in is your top risk and the decision you need; anything that would have been open-ended TA coaching happens async between check-ins. Your demo makes the same claim as your contribution line, out loud, in front of the people who would know if it weren't true.

A video replaces the live demo in two cases only: you miss the check-in, or your TA cannot meet your team that week. Record it on [media.oregonstate.edu](https://media.oregonstate.edu/) with captions on, share it unlisted, and give one timestamp per student so each segment can be found and scored on its own (a student who missed alone records only their own segment). Link it in the sprint note, or in a comment on your submission if the note is already in, within 48 hours of the scheduled check-in.

The all-hands [demo days](/assignments/demo-day/) are a different thing from this cadence: the whole class in one room, partners invited, and your team takes one of the five across the year.

## Sprint Notes N: Individual Contribution

<AssignmentMeta submission="Individual, nothing to submit" due="With each sprint note" weight="Half of each sprint's 2%, defaulting to full credit" />

A student's individual half is reduced when the sprint's evidence (contribution lines, repo record, review activity) shows no meaningful contribution. Deductions are flagged by the team's TA with the evidence noted, and confirmed by an instructor following the corroboration process on the [partner evaluation page](/assignments/project-partner-evaluation/); no deduction lands without a written evidence note you can see and respond to. You have five business days from notification to respond with evidence; an instructor makes the final call. Missing your PR-per-sprint norm once with an explanation in the note costs you nothing; a pattern with no explanation is what reduces your individual half.
