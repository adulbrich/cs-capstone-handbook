Every two-week [sprint](https://scrumguides.org/scrum-guide.html) ends with a one-page sprint note, due by Sunday of the sprint's second week, and a demo of working software at your TA check-in that week. The note is evidence plus judgment, not prose: if your repository and tracker are in order, producing it takes 15 minutes. The [sprint guide](/guides/sprint/) covers how to plan, run, and review a sprint; the windows below are when this course's sprints fall.

| Term | Sprints | Sprint windows |
|---|---|---|
| Fall | 1 to 4 | Weeks 3 to 4, 5 to 6, 7 to 8, 9 to 10 |
| Winter | 5 to 9 | Weeks 1 to 2, 3 to 4, 5 to 6, 7 to 8, 9 to 10 |
| Spring | 10 to 12 | Weeks 1 to 2, 3 to 4, 5 to 6 |

## What You Produce

The sprint note is one page with four items:

1. **Working software evidence**: a URL, tagged release, or demo video, plus the CI run. If nothing works yet, say so plainly and say why.
2. **What got done, and who**: the work the team finished this sprint and, if you choose, the work still in progress, each entry concrete and consequential, with its owner, in the shape below.
3. **Top risk and decisions**: the risk ranked first in your `docs/risks.md`, linked at the sprint's last commit, however small it is. The register ranks risks by threat to shipping. Then one decision the team needs, linked to where it is being made and naming who makes it (the partner, the instruction team, or the team itself), or a plain statement that no decision is needed this sprint. Last, the previous note's decision, as [Decisions and who chaired them](#decisions-and-who-chaired-them) describes.
4. **Judgment layer** (about five lines, human-written): what the team learned this sprint, naming at least one change to the plan it caused, with its link (a re-scoped story, a re-ranked risk, a revised estimate), or saying plainly that the plan holds and why.

An early sprint can be light on code, or have none, and that's fine when the note says where you are and why. For example:

- No code yet, by agreement: the partner asked for a research and planning phase first. Done: the comparison of existing tools the partner reviewed, the requirements draft they approved, notes from interviews with their staff.
- No code yet, by agreement: the partner wanted the design settled before any build. Done: the architecture the partner reviewed, [ADRs](/guides/adr/) (architecture decision records) for the storage and hosting decisions, a data model, the interface contract with the partner's existing system.
- No working software yet: the open-source project you contribute to builds and passes its tests on every member's machine, and the first pull request, a documentation fix, is in review.
- The pipeline runs end to end on synthetic data; the real data waits on the data agreement, whose owner is named under Top risk.
- A clickable prototype only, because the partner's system access hasn't been granted; the request is linked.

Each sprint, update the [living docs](/assignments/repo-checkpoints/#what-you-produce) for what the sprint changed, starting with `docs/requirements.md`, `docs/design.md`, and `docs/risks.md`. In fall and winter the next repo checkpoint checks them; in spring they go to whoever continues the project, through the [Project Handoff](/assignments/project-handoff/#4-the-living-docs).

**AI use:** you may generate the evidence with AI from your own repo history and board, the entries included. Two things must be human-written, because they are the parts anyone reads: the judgment layer, and each student's [How we checked it](#what-got-done-and-who) sentence on the entry they mark as their most consequential. Start from the [sprint note template](/sprint-note-template.md).

### What counts as "done"

Concrete means someone could go and look at it. Consequential means the project is meaningfully further along because of it. It does not have to be code. A partner agreement reached, a dataset cleared for use, a deployment pipeline that now works, a real-user session run and written up, a flaky test suite made reliable: all of these are done things worth naming. So are a literature or prior-work review the partner discussed with you, a requirements document the partner approved, an architecture or design document the partner reviewed, a design decision recorded as an ADR, a prototype the partner tried, and a development environment that builds from `README.md` for every member. Work that is not code counts when someone can check it, wherever it lives: findings, interview notes, a requirements section, an RFC section.

What does not count is activity without a result. "Worked on the API", "researched options", "had meetings", and "made progress on the frontend" describe how time was spent, not what exists now that did not exist two weeks ago.

Everything in this list should be something you can show. The demo and this list are the same content in two formats.

### What got done, and who

Every sprint, every student owns at least one piece of work that someone else accepted. That is the contribution minimum, and [Individual Contribution](#sprint-notes-n-individual-contribution-1) says how the evidence for it is read.

Write two lists, each entry as a table row or under its own subheading (the template uses subheadings):

- **Done**: work someone else took this sprint, meaning the pull request approved and merged, the issue or ticket closed with its outcome, the document approved, including by your partner in their own system.
- **In progress**, if you want your started work seen: work still open at sprint close, in review or underway. An entry moves to Done in the sprint someone accepts it.

Each entry has:

- **What now exists**, or for work in progress what is being built, linked to where it is written up. A code change is its pull request; other work is the issue, ticket, or document where it lives, in the tracker your [`CONTRIBUTING.md`](/assignments/team-charter/#required-in-contributingmd) names. A board card or a commit list is not a write-up. Where a grader can't open it, describe it instead, as the note under [What You Submit](#what-you-submit) says.
- **Its owner**: the student who authored or drove it and answered review, and on Done, saw it accepted.
- **Who reviewed and accepted it** on Done, or **its status**, in review or underway, on In progress. Either takes a reviewer note, with the reviewer's name, where a review took judgment (below).
- **How we checked it**, on each student's most consequential Done entry, which they mark. The owner writes this sentence themselves: for code, the test or the run; for research, a decision, or a document, what it was checked against and who confirmed it.

Work finished in an earlier sprint and routine upkeep (a formatter run, a lockfile bump, a rename, a ticket moved on the board) go on neither list. One line closes them: the students who own nothing on either list this sprint. A student named there doesn't fail the team's item; whether it costs them anything is their individual contribution's question.

Your part of the note is then three things: the entries you own, the reviewer notes you wrote, and the How we checked it sentence on your marked entry.

A routine approval, a bare "LGTM" included, is the reviewer's name and nothing else. A review that took judgment (code that was hard to get right, a design decision, an ADR, an RFC, a test plan, a data change) gets a reviewer note naming the change you asked for ("asked Priya to handle the empty-cart case in checkout") or what you checked ("ran the migration against a copy of the seed data and compared row counts"). Where an AI reviewer ran on the change, the note also says what you checked that the tool could not. There is no minimum per sprint, but in spring your [defense](/assignments/defense/#rubric) reads these notes for its reviewer-side question, and a student with none all spring has nothing to show there.

[Google's code review standard](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/standard.html) is worth reading on where that bar sits: approve once the change improves the overall code health of the system, even when it is not perfect, and say what would need to change if it does not.

:::note[How the numbers are read]
Entries and reviews are evidence where "everyone contributed" is not. Volume is easy to inflate and easy to misread, so three rules bound it.

Routine upkeep is not an entry. A formatter run, a lockfile bump, a rename, a ticket moved on the board, a generated file, a dependency update: all normal work, none of it a signal about contribution. Don't pad the list with it, and graders won't count it. Lines of code are not counted either: a lint pass can move ten thousand lines and mean nothing.

More is not scored. One Done entry meets the minimum, and how each sprint's individual contribution is scored is under [Individual Contribution](#sprint-notes-n-individual-contribution-1).

The sentence outranks the count. A student with one entry who can say how the team checked it is contributing more than a student with six entries and nothing to say about any of them.
:::

### Decisions and who chaired them

Each note reports the previous note's decision: what was decided and who chaired it, meaning who drove it to a conclusion, or that it is still open and why. If the previous note said no decision was needed, there is nothing to report. Don't invent a decision to fill item 3; a plain "no decision is needed this sprint" passes. The note may also name any other decision the team concluded this sprint and who chaired it.

Over a term every student should chair at least one decision, as your [charter](/assignments/team-charter/#1-roles) commits to. This record is what your [defense](/assignments/defense/) role criterion is checked against, so it saves you reconstructing your own leadership from memory at your defense.

## Demo Cadence

Every sprint, your team demos live to your Teaching Assistant (TA) in a check-in during the sprint-note week, in person or over Zoom, at a time your team and your TA agree. One team, one TA, nobody else's team in the room. Every student demos their own work: pick one entry you own on either of this sprint's lists, open it, show the change, and run it. It cannot be the artifact you showed at the last check-in: nothing new to show means nothing was done that sprint. Your TA follows up with a question about what you showed, such as which lines are yours or what your reviewer asked you to change.

Two to three minutes each, so 6 to 12 minutes of a 25-to-30-minute check-in for a team of three or four. The rest of the check-in is your top risk and the decision you need; anything that would have been open-ended TA coaching happens async between check-ins. Your demo makes the same claim as your entries in the note, out loud, in front of the people who would know if it weren't true.

**Sprint Notes 1:** the fall week 4 check-in is the week before [Repo Checkpoint 1](/assignments/repo-checkpoints/#repo-checkpoint-1-set-up). After the demos, the time for your top risk and decision goes to a review that is not graded: your TA goes through what Repo Checkpoint 1 asks for against your repository, so you find what is missing with a week left to fix it.

A video replaces the live demo in two cases only: you miss the check-in, or your TA cannot meet your team that week. Record it on [media.oregonstate.edu](https://media.oregonstate.edu/) with captions on, share it unlisted, and give one timestamp per student so each segment can be found and scored on its own (a student who missed alone records only their own segment). Link it in the sprint note, or in a comment on your submission if the note is already in, within 48 hours of the scheduled check-in.

The all-hands [demo days](/assignments/demo-day/) are a different thing from this cadence: the whole class in one room, and your team takes one of the five across the year.

## What You Submit

:::note[If your project is under NDA]
Where a grader can't open your repository or your partner's system, describe each entry in place of its link: what now exists or is being built, with partner specifics replaced by neutral placeholders ("the partner's internal system", "the dataset"), following the [sanitized-version rule](/introduction/ip-nda-and-licensing/#teams-under-an-nda). At the check-in, with your screen shared, your TA opens the work behind one owned entry per student, a Done one where you have one, the reviewer notes each student wrote, and the demo artifact, and marks each in the check-in record. A described entry your TA didn't open there doesn't check out. When the check-in falls after the note is due, your TA grades those items after the meeting.
:::

## Rubric

### Sprint Notes N

Each note is checked pass/fail per criterion, 20 points each, and two criteria, What got done and Ownership and review, grade the same two lists; the [rubric conventions](/assignments/introduction/#rubric-conventions) list it among the rubrics that use other bands.

### Sprint Notes N: Individual Contribution

<RubricTable csv={contributionCsv} sourceLabel="canvas/assignments/individual-contribution/individual-contribution-rubric.csv" />

Your individual half defaults to full credit. A student's individual half is reduced when the sprint's evidence (their entries, the pull request, issue, or document each one links, review activity) falls short of the contribution minimum. Deductions are flagged by the team's TA with an evidence note, then confirmed by an instructor; no deduction lands without a written evidence note you can see and respond to. You have five business days from notification to respond with evidence; an instructor makes the final call. Each sprint is scored on its own and as a whole, from the first one, in one of the rubric's four bands: the grader picks a band, never a number between two. One accepted piece scores Full however much else of yours is still open or in review; In review and Thin apply only to a sprint with none. Being blocked is not a reason to contribute nothing, since there is other work to pick up, unless the whole team is blocked.

Two other sources can raise a concern about your contribution: the [Defense](/assignments/defense/), which spot-checks ownership claims live, and your partner's surveys, which can flag an individual concern and start an [individual review](/assignments/project-partner-evaluation/#instructor-review) of your partner evaluation score.

If you join your first team mid-term, the sprints before you joined score full credit on your individual contribution, and your defense and RFC draw on the work since you joined. If you switch teams, each sprint is scored on the team you were on that sprint.
